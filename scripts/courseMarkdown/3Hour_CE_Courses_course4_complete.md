# Course: It Takes a Village: Collaborative Care and Ethical Consultation
## 3.0 CE Hours | Ethics/Clinical Practice

---

## COURSE INFORMATION

**Course Title:** It Takes a Village: Collaborative Care and Ethical Consultation

**Course Description:** The African proverb "It takes a village to raise a child" captures a fundamental truth about human flourishing—we cannot do it alone. The same applies to mental health treatment. Complex clients require collaborative care involving multiple providers, disciplines, and systems. This comprehensive 3-hour course examines the art and ethics of consultation, coordination, and collaboration. From seeking consultation to providing it, from navigating referrals to coordinating care teams, participants will develop competencies essential for effective practice in today's integrated healthcare landscape.

**Target Audience:** Licensed Professional Counselors, Licensed Mental Health Counselors, Marriage and Family Therapists, Clinical Social Workers, and counselors-in-training.

**Credit Hours:** 3.0 CE Hours (Ethics/Clinical Practice)

**Course Level:** Intermediate

---

## LEARNING OBJECTIVES

Upon successful completion of this course, participants will be able to:

1. **Distinguish** between consultation, supervision, and collaborative care models and apply each appropriately.

2. **Implement** ethical procedures for seeking and providing clinical consultation in accordance with ACA and state requirements.

3. **Navigate** the referral process effectively, including when to refer, how to identify appropriate resources, and warm handoff techniques.

4. **Coordinate** care with other providers while maintaining appropriate confidentiality and professional boundaries.

5. **Collaborate** effectively within interdisciplinary teams, understanding different professional roles and communication styles.

6. **Apply** ethical decision-making principles to common dilemmas in consultation and collaboration.

7. **Utilize** consultation as an ongoing professional development and self-care practice.

8. **Develop** systems for maintaining communication and documentation across multiple providers.

## NARRATIVE SCRIPT

### INTRODUCTION: WHY THE VILLAGE MATTERS (10 minutes)

Welcome to "It Takes a Village: Collaborative Care and Ethical Consultation." The proverb that inspires our title reminds us that complex human development requires a community—no single caregiver can provide everything a child needs.

The same is true in mental health treatment. Our clients present with complex, multifaceted needs that often exceed what any single provider can address:

- The client with depression who also needs medication management
- The trauma survivor who needs counseling, psychiatric care, and case management
- The adolescent whose treatment involves parents, school counselors, and pediatricians
- The client with chronic illness whose mental health intersects with medical care
- The couple whose marital therapy is complicated by one partner's individual issues

We cannot be all things to all clients. Ethical, effective practice requires knowing when and how to consult, refer, and collaborate.

Over the next three hours, we'll explore:
- The foundations of consultation
- Seeking consultation effectively
- Providing consultation to others
- The art of the referral
- Coordinating care across providers
- Working in interdisciplinary teams
- Documentation and communication systems
- Ethical considerations throughout

By the end, you'll have concrete skills for building and participating in the "village" your clients need.

### MODULE 1: FOUNDATIONS OF CONSULTATION (25 minutes)

**Defining Consultation**

Consultation is a professional relationship in which one professional (the consultee) seeks assistance from another (the consultant) regarding a work-related problem. Key features include:

*Voluntary*: The consultee chooses to seek consultation and is free to accept or reject recommendations.

*Expert-based*: The consultant brings specialized knowledge or perspective the consultee lacks.

*Problem-focused*: Consultation addresses specific professional challenges rather than personal issues.

*Time-limited*: Unlike supervision, consultation typically addresses discrete situations rather than ongoing oversight.

*Responsibility retained*: The consultee retains clinical and ethical responsibility for the case.

**Consultation vs. Supervision**

These terms are sometimes used interchangeably but have important differences:

*Supervision*:
- Usually mandated (for licensure, employment)
- Hierarchical relationship with evaluative component
- Supervisor shares legal and ethical responsibility
- Ongoing over extended period
- Supervisor has authority over supervisee's practice

*Consultation*:
- Usually voluntary
- Peer or expert relationship without evaluation
- Consultant doesn't share case responsibility
- Usually focused on specific situations
- Consultee retains autonomous decision-making

**Types of Consultation**

*Case consultation*: Seeking input on a specific client situation. "I have a client presenting with X. How might I approach this?"

*Collegial consultation*: Informal discussion with peers about clinical challenges. Less structured but valuable for processing and problem-solving.

*Expert consultation*: Seeking input from someone with specialized expertise—a particular diagnosis, population, or treatment modality.

*Ethics consultation*: Seeking guidance on ethical dilemmas. May involve ethics committee, professional organization, or ethics experts.

*Legal consultation*: Seeking guidance on legal questions from attorneys familiar with mental health law.

**When to Seek Consultation**

Consultation is indicated when:
- You're uncertain about diagnosis, treatment approach, or clinical decision
- The case is outside your usual scope or expertise
- Ethical dilemmas arise
- Legal questions arise
- You're experiencing significant countertransference
- High-risk situations emerge (suicidality, violence)
- Treatment isn't progressing as expected
- You need support processing difficult cases

The ACA Code of Ethics (C.2.e) states: "Counselors consult with other counselors or related professionals when they have questions regarding their ethical obligations or professional practice."

### MODULE 2: SEEKING CONSULTATION EFFECTIVELY (30 minutes)

**Before You Consult**

Preparation enhances consultation effectiveness:

*Clarify your question*: What specifically do you want input on? Diagnosis? Treatment approach? Ethical dilemma? The clearer your question, the more useful the consultation.

*Gather relevant information*: Have case details available—history, current presentation, what you've tried, what you're considering.

*Consider confidentiality*: What information is necessary to share? What can remain private? Have releases if needed for identifying consultation.

*Identify appropriate consultants*: Who has the expertise you need? Who do you trust?

**Choosing Consultants**

Consider:
- Does this person have relevant expertise?
- Do they have experience with similar situations?
- Can they be objective, or are they too close to the situation?
- Do you trust their judgment?
- Are there any conflicts of interest?

For clinical consultation, colleagues with more experience or specialized training are often ideal. For ethical dilemmas, ethics committees or ethics-focused consultants are appropriate. For legal questions, attorneys specializing in mental health law are necessary.

**Presenting the Case**

Present cases efficiently:

1. *Brief context*: Client demographics, presenting problem, treatment history
2. *Current situation*: What's happening now that prompted consultation?
3. *Your thinking*: What have you considered? What approaches have you tried?
4. *Your question*: What specifically do you want input on?

Avoid data dumps that overwhelm the consultant. Focus on information relevant to your question.

**Receiving Input**

*Listen openly*: Even if suggestions differ from your thinking, hear them fully.

*Ask clarifying questions*: "Can you say more about why you'd approach it that way?"

*Consider multiple perspectives*: If you consult several people, value different viewpoints.

*Remember autonomy*: You retain decision-making responsibility. Consultation informs but doesn't dictate.

**Documentation**

Document consultation:
- Date and consultant's credentials
- Issue consulted about
- Summary of input received
- Your clinical reasoning and decisions

This protects you legally and supports continuity of care.

### MODULE 3: PROVIDING CONSULTATION (25 minutes)

**Roles and Boundaries**

When providing consultation:

*Clarify the relationship*: Is this supervision or consultation? Formal or informal? What's the scope?

*Clarify expectations*: What is the consultee looking for? Information? Validation? Direction?

*Maintain appropriate boundaries*: Don't take over the case. Don't become a shadow therapist.

*Respect autonomy*: Offer perspectives; don't dictate.

**Effective Consultation Skills**

*Ask before telling*: "What have you considered?" "What's your gut telling you?" The consultee often has relevant instincts.

*Listen fully*: Understand the situation before offering input.

*Share thinking, not just conclusions*: "Here's my reasoning..." helps the consultee learn, not just receive answers.

*Acknowledge uncertainty*: "I'm not sure, but here's what I'd consider..."

*Offer multiple options*: When appropriate, present alternatives rather than single solutions.

*Check understanding*: "Does that address what you were asking?" "Is that helpful?"

**Ethical Considerations**

When providing consultation:

*Competence*: Only consult within your expertise. If the question is outside your scope, say so and suggest appropriate resources.

*Confidentiality*: Handle case information appropriately. If you're concerned about the consultee's practice, consider your ethical obligations.

*Conflicts of interest*: Disclose any conflicts. Avoid consulting on situations where you're not objective.

*Documentation*: Consider what documentation is appropriate on your end.

**Paid Consultation**

Some clinicians provide paid consultation services. Considerations include:
- Clear contracts defining scope, fees, and expectations
- Clarifying that consultation doesn't create a treatment relationship with the client
- Appropriate professional liability coverage
- Documentation of the consultation relationship

### MODULE 4: THE ART OF REFERRAL (30 minutes)

**When to Refer**

Referral is indicated when:

*Scope of competence*: The client needs services outside your expertise.

*Level of care*: The client needs a higher or different level of care than you can provide.

*Practical limitations*: You lack availability, or the client's location or financial situation requires different resources.

*Client welfare*: Continued treatment with you isn't serving the client's best interests.

*Ethical conflicts*: The situation creates ethical conflicts that cannot be resolved.

The ACA Code of Ethics (A.11.b) states: "If counselors determine an inability to be of professional assistance to clients, they avoid entering or continuing counseling relationships."

**Identifying Appropriate Resources**

Build and maintain referral resources:

*Network actively*: Know providers in your area—psychiatrists, specialists, agencies, support groups.

*Evaluate resources*: Not all resources are equal. Know providers' strengths, specialties, and limitations.

*Maintain updated information*: Contact information, insurance acceptance, current availability.

*Consider client needs*: Match referrals to client needs—location, finances, cultural fit, specific expertise.

**The Referral Conversation**

How you discuss referral matters:

*Frame appropriately*: "I want to make sure you're getting everything you need. I think adding/switching to X could help because..."

*Avoid rejection language*: Not "I can't help you" but "I think you'd benefit from someone who specializes in..."

*Normalize*: Referral isn't failure; it's appropriate care.

*Provide specific resources*: Don't just say "you should see a psychiatrist." Provide names, numbers, and information.

*Discuss transition*: Will you continue seeing them during transition? Will you be available for consultation with the new provider?

**Warm Handoffs**

A "warm handoff" involves direct connection with the receiving provider:

*With consent*, contact the receiving provider directly:
- Introduce the client and their needs
- Share relevant history and clinical information
- Discuss any concerns or special considerations
- Facilitate the client's first contact

Warm handoffs dramatically increase follow-through compared to simply giving clients information.

**Following Up**

After referral:
- Follow up to see if the client connected with the resource
- If they didn't, explore barriers and problem-solve
- If they did, determine your ongoing role (if any)

### MODULE 5: COORDINATING CARE (30 minutes)

**The Need for Coordination**

Many clients see multiple providers:
- Primary care physicians
- Psychiatrists
- Individual therapists
- Group therapists
- Case managers
- Specialized providers (substance abuse, eating disorders)
- School counselors
- Probation officers

Without coordination, treatment can be fragmented, contradictory, or redundant.

**Releases of Information**

Coordination requires appropriate releases:

*Obtain proper authorization*: Written releases meeting HIPAA and state requirements.

*Specify scope*: What information can be shared? With whom? For what purpose? For how long?

*Clarify direction*: Is the release for you to share, to receive, or both?

*Renew as needed*: Releases expire. Maintain current authorizations.

**What to Communicate**

Share information relevant to coordinated care:
- Treatment focus and goals
- Relevant history affecting current treatment
- Current interventions and approaches
- Safety concerns
- Medication information when relevant
- Progress and challenges

Don't share unnecessary personal details. Clients consent to coordination, not gossip.

**Communication Methods**

*Written reports*: Formal summaries useful for complex situations or documentation needs.

*Phone/video consultations*: Allow dialogue and clarification. Efficient for complex situations.

*Secure messaging*: Quick updates and coordination. Must use HIPAA-compliant platforms.

*Treatment team meetings*: Regular meetings for complex cases with multiple providers.

**Managing Disagreements**

Providers sometimes disagree. When this happens:
- Focus on client welfare
- Seek to understand the other perspective
- Look for common ground
- Clarify respective roles and expertise
- If necessary, include the client in discussions
- Document disagreements and reasoning

### MODULE 6: INTERDISCIPLINARY COLLABORATION (25 minutes)

**Understanding Different Disciplines**

Effective collaboration requires understanding different roles:

*Psychiatrists*: Medical doctors specializing in mental health. Prescribe medications, provide psychiatric evaluation.

*Psychologists*: Doctoral-level training in psychology. Psychological testing, therapy, research.

*Licensed Professional Counselors*: Master's-level counseling training. Therapy, counseling, assessment.

*Clinical Social Workers*: Social work training with clinical focus. Therapy, case management, systems navigation.

*Marriage and Family Therapists*: Specialized training in family systems. Couples and family therapy.

*Psychiatric Nurse Practitioners*: Nursing background with psychiatric specialization. Can prescribe medications.

*Case Managers*: Coordinate services, help navigate systems, connect to resources.

Each brings different training, perspective, and scope of practice.

**Communication Across Disciplines**

Different disciplines have different languages and cultures:

*Medical providers*: Often prefer concise, factual communication. May think in diagnostic and symptom terms.

*Social workers*: May emphasize systems, resources, and social context.

*Counselors*: May emphasize therapeutic relationship and process.

Understanding these differences enhances communication:
- Adapt your communication style to your audience
- Clarify terminology that may mean different things to different disciplines
- Respect different expertise and perspectives
- Educate about your role when needed

**Team Meetings**

Effective team meetings:
- Have clear purpose and agenda
- Include all relevant providers
- Allocate time appropriately
- Focus on coordination, not competition
- Result in clear action items
- Are documented appropriately

Your role: Come prepared, participate actively, advocate for your clinical perspective, remain collaborative.

**Managing Interprofessional Tensions**

Tensions arise from:
- Different perspectives and approaches
- Scope of practice confusion
- Resource competition
- Historical interprofessional biases
- Power dynamics

Manage tensions by:
- Focusing on client welfare
- Respecting different expertise
- Communicating clearly and professionally
- Addressing conflicts directly rather than avoiding
- Seeking supervision or consultation when needed

### MODULE 7: ETHICAL AND PRACTICAL CONSIDERATIONS (15 minutes)

**Confidentiality in Collaboration**

Balancing coordination and confidentiality:
- Share only what's necessary and authorized
- Obtain specific, informed consent for information sharing
- Clarify what information will be shared and with whom
- Respect the client's right to limit disclosure
- Document authorization and disclosures

**Informed Consent**

Clients should understand:
- Who is involved in their care
- What information will be shared
- The purpose of coordination
- Their right to limit disclosure
- How to raise concerns about coordination

**Documentation**

Maintain documentation of:
- Releases of information and their scope
- Communications with other providers
- Consultations sought and received
- Treatment team decisions
- Client involvement in coordinated care

**Consultation as Self-Care**

Regular consultation:
- Prevents isolation
- Provides support for difficult cases
- Enhances clinical skill
- Reduces liability risk
- Supports professional development

Build consultation into your practice routine, not just crisis response.

### CONCLUSION: BUILDING YOUR VILLAGE (10 minutes)

We began with the proverb: "It takes a village to raise a child." Your clients need a village too—and you are part of that village, not the whole of it.

Effective practitioners:

*Know their limits*: We can't be all things to all clients. Knowing when to consult, refer, and collaborate is strength, not weakness.

*Build relationships*: Develop networks of trusted colleagues, consultants, and referral resources before you need them.

*Communicate effectively*: Clear, professional, timely communication enables coordination.

*Respect other roles*: Different disciplines bring different strengths. Collaboration beats competition.

*Maintain boundaries*: Coordination serves clients. Keep confidentiality, consent, and client welfare central.

*Use consultation routinely*: Not just in crisis, but as ongoing practice.

The village model recognizes that we're stronger together than alone. Your clients benefit when you're connected to resources, supported by consultation, and collaborating effectively with other providers.

Thank you for your commitment to building villages that support healing.
