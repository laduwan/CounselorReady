# Course: Between a Rock and a Hard Place: Navigating Ethical Dilemmas in Clinical Practice
## 3.0 CE Hours | Ethics

---

## COURSE INFORMATION

**Course Title:** Between a Rock and a Hard Place: Navigating Ethical Dilemmas in Clinical Practice

**Course Description:** Ethical practice is straightforward—until it isn't. Real clinical work often places counselors "between a rock and a hard place," facing situations where ethical principles conflict, where the right answer isn't clear, and where every option carries risk. This comprehensive 3-hour course prepares counselors to navigate genuine ethical dilemmas—situations where reasonable professionals might disagree and where ethical principles pull in different directions. Through case analysis and decision-making frameworks, participants will develop competencies for making sound ethical decisions even in the most challenging circumstances.

**Target Audience:** Licensed Professional Counselors, Licensed Mental Health Counselors, Marriage and Family Therapists, Clinical Social Workers, and counselors-in-training.

**Credit Hours:** 3.0 CE Hours (Ethics)

**Course Level:** Intermediate to Advanced

---

## LEARNING OBJECTIVES

Upon successful completion of this course, participants will be able to:

1. **Distinguish** between ethical violations, ethical issues, and true ethical dilemmas requiring judgment.

2. **Apply** systematic ethical decision-making models to complex clinical situations.

3. **Analyze** situations involving conflicts between ethical principles including autonomy, beneficence, nonmaleficence, and justice.

4. **Navigate** confidentiality dilemmas including duty to warn, court involvement, and third-party requests.

5. **Address** boundary dilemmas in rural, small community, and specialized practice contexts.

6. **Manage** dilemmas involving client autonomy including treatment refusal, capacity questions, and high-risk choices.

7. **Resolve** conflicts between ethical obligations and legal requirements or institutional policies.

8. **Utilize** consultation and documentation practices that support ethical decision-making.

## NARRATIVE SCRIPT

### INTRODUCTION: THE HARD PLACE (10 minutes)

Welcome to "Between a Rock and a Hard Place: Navigating Ethical Dilemmas in Clinical Practice." The expression captures what genuine ethical dilemmas feel like—trapped between two difficult options, with no escape that avoids harm.

Consider these situations:

A client tells you he intends to leave his wife—but asks you not to tell her during their couples therapy session later that day. Do you keep his confidence or protect the integrity of the couples work?

A suicidal client refuses hospitalization, insists she has the right to make her own decisions, and threatens to end the therapeutic relationship if you violate her autonomy. Do you respect autonomy or protect life?

A court orders you to release records for a custody evaluation. Your client, who disclosed childhood abuse in therapy, begs you not to—she fears her ex-husband will use it against her. Do you comply with the court or protect your client?

These aren't situations with easy answers. They're genuine dilemmas—situations where ethical principles conflict, where reasonable professionals might disagree, and where every choice carries risk.

This course won't give you simple answers. Instead, it will equip you with:
- Frameworks for analyzing dilemmas
- Understanding of competing ethical principles
- Strategies for navigating common difficult situations
- Consultation and documentation practices
- The humility to acknowledge the difficulty while acting responsibly

Let's learn to navigate between the rock and the hard place.

### MODULE 1: UNDERSTANDING ETHICAL DILEMMAS (25 minutes)

**Ethical Violations vs. Ethical Dilemmas**

Not every ethical challenge is a dilemma. Distinguish:

*Ethical violations*: Clear breaches of ethical standards. Having sex with a client isn't a dilemma—it's wrong, period.

*Ethical issues*: Situations requiring ethical attention but with clear guidance. Maintaining confidentiality generally isn't a dilemma—the code tells you what to do.

*Ethical dilemmas*: Situations where ethical principles conflict, where the code doesn't provide clear direction, where reasonable professionals might disagree.

This course focuses on true dilemmas—the situations that keep you up at night.

**When Principles Conflict**

Ethical dilemmas often involve conflict between fundamental principles:

*Autonomy vs. Beneficence*: The client's right to make their own decisions vs. your desire to help them.

*Autonomy vs. Nonmaleficence*: The client's choices vs. preventing harm (to self or others).

*Confidentiality vs. Duty to Warn*: Protecting privacy vs. protecting potential victims.

*Individual Client vs. Others*: One client's interests vs. family members, other clients, or society.

*Ethics vs. Law*: Ethical obligations vs. legal requirements.

When principles conflict, you're between a rock and a hard place.

**The Limits of Codes**

Ethical codes are essential but insufficient for dilemmas:

- Codes provide principles but can't anticipate every situation
- Codes tell you what matters but not always what to prioritize
- Different codes may provide different guidance
- Reasonable interpretation of codes can vary

Codes frame the conversation but don't eliminate judgment.

**Moral Distress**

Ethical dilemmas cause moral distress—the painful feeling when you can't do what you believe is right or must choose between conflicting "rights."

Moral distress is:
- Normal in genuinely difficult situations
- Not a sign of incompetence
- Deserving of attention and support

If you never feel moral distress, you may not be recognizing the dilemmas.

### MODULE 2: ETHICAL DECISION-MAKING MODELS (25 minutes)

**Why Use a Model?**

Systematic decision-making models help by:
- Ensuring comprehensive consideration of relevant factors
- Reducing bias and emotional reactivity
- Creating documentation of reasoning
- Supporting defensible decisions
- Slowing down when instinct might rush

No model guarantees right answers, but models improve process.

**The Forester-Miller and Davis Model**

A practical seven-step model:

1. *Identify the problem*: What specifically is the ethical issue?
2. *Apply the ACA Code of Ethics*: What guidance does the code provide?
3. *Determine the nature and dimensions*: What principles are involved? Who is affected?
4. *Generate potential courses of action*: What options exist? Be creative.
5. *Consider consequences*: For each option, what are likely outcomes?
6. *Evaluate the selected course of action*: Does it pass ethical tests?
7. *Implement the course of action*: Take action, document, and evaluate.

**Ethical Tests**

Several "tests" help evaluate potential decisions:

*Justice test*: Would I make the same decision regardless of who was involved?

*Universalizability test*: Would I recommend this action to any professional in similar circumstances?

*Publicity test*: Would I be comfortable if this decision were publicly known?

*Care test*: Does this decision reflect genuine care for all involved?

*Professional test*: Is this what an ethical professional would do?

If a decision fails multiple tests, reconsider.

**The Role of Intuition**

Intuition matters but shouldn't be trusted blindly:

- Intuition often reflects accumulated experience
- But intuition can also reflect bias, avoidance, or self-interest
- Use models to check intuition
- If your gut says one thing and the model says another, examine both

**Documentation of Ethical Decision-Making**

Document your process:
- The dilemma faced
- Factors considered
- Options identified
- Rationale for your decision
- Consultations obtained
- Actions taken

Documentation protects you and supports learning.

### MODULE 3: CONFIDENTIALITY DILEMMAS (30 minutes)

**The Centrality of Confidentiality**

Confidentiality is foundational to counseling. Without it, clients won't disclose and therapy can't work. Breaking confidentiality is always serious.

Yet exceptions exist—and navigating them creates dilemmas.

**Duty to Warn/Protect**

When clients threaten violence against identifiable third parties, duty to warn/protect may apply:

The dilemma: You have duties to both client confidentiality and third-party safety. These conflict.

Navigating it:
- Know your state's specific law—requirements vary
- Assess the threat carefully—is it serious and credible?
- Consider whether intervention with the client could reduce risk
- Take the least intrusive action that addresses the danger
- Document your assessment and reasoning
- Consult when possible

**Suspected Abuse Reporting**

Mandatory reporting of child or elder abuse creates confidentiality dilemmas:

The dilemma: Breaking confidentiality may harm the therapeutic relationship and the client, but failing to report harms potential victims and violates law.

Navigating it:
- Know your mandatory reporting requirements
- Report when required—this isn't optional
- Inform clients of reporting obligations early in treatment
- When possible, involve the client in the reporting process
- Continue therapeutic work around the report
- Document thoroughly

**Court Orders and Subpoenas**

When courts demand records:

The dilemma: You have confidentiality obligations to clients and legal obligations to courts. These can conflict.

Navigating it:
- Distinguish subpoenas (attorney requests) from court orders (judge commands)
- Assert privilege on the client's behalf initially
- Seek client's informed consent for release
- If court orders release, comply only as narrowly as possible
- Consult legal counsel when appropriate
- Document everything

**Third-Party Requests**

Parents wanting information about adolescent clients. Employers wanting fitness-for-duty opinions. Family members wanting updates.

The dilemma: Others have legitimate interests in client information, but clients have confidentiality rights.

Navigating it:
- Clarify who your client is
- Discuss confidentiality limits at the outset
- Obtain clear consent for any disclosures
- Release minimum necessary information
- Consider the client's interests in all disclosures

### MODULE 4: BOUNDARY DILEMMAS (30 minutes)

**The Purpose of Boundaries**

Boundaries protect:
- The client from exploitation
- The therapeutic frame
- The therapist's objectivity
- The professional relationship

Rigid, absolute boundaries are the ideal. But reality is messier.

**Small Community Practice**

In small, rural, or specialized communities, avoiding all dual relationships may be impossible:

The dilemma: Strict boundary maintenance may be impossible when your client is also your child's teacher, your neighbor, or the only plumber in town.

Navigating it:
- Anticipate dual relationships and discuss with clients proactively
- Assess whether dual relationships will harm the therapeutic work
- Maintain the clearest boundaries possible within constraints
- Consult with colleagues
- Document your reasoning
- Prioritize the therapeutic relationship in role conflicts

**Online and Social Media**

The digital world creates boundary dilemmas:

The dilemma: Clients may seek to connect on social media, discover personal information online, or blur professional boundaries in digital spaces.

Navigating it:
- Have clear policies about online interaction
- Discuss digital boundaries as part of informed consent
- Protect your own privacy online
- Don't "friend" clients or engage personally on social media
- Consider the searchability of your personal information

**Gifts and Exchanges**

Clients may offer gifts or request exchanges:

The dilemma: Rejecting gifts may harm the relationship or be culturally insensitive; accepting them may create obligation or boundary problems.

Navigating it:
- Consider the value, meaning, and context of gifts
- Small, culturally appropriate tokens may be acceptable
- Large, expensive, or frequent gifts are concerning
- Process the meaning of gift-giving therapeutically
- Document significant gifts and your handling of them

**Post-Termination Relationships**

What about relationships after therapy ends?

The dilemma: Former clients are no longer clients, but the therapeutic relationship creates lasting power dynamics.

Navigating it:
- Sexual relationships with former clients remain prohibited (most codes specify minimum time periods)
- Friendships with former clients should be approached very cautiously
- Consider the nature and duration of treatment
- Consider the vulnerability of the client
- Consider time elapsed since termination
- When in doubt, maintain boundaries

### MODULE 5: AUTONOMY DILEMMAS (25 minutes)

**The Principle of Autonomy**

Autonomy—the client's right to make their own decisions—is fundamental. We're not in the business of controlling people.

Yet autonomy has limits when choices cause serious harm.

**Treatment Refusal**

Clients may refuse recommended treatment:

The dilemma: Respecting autonomy means accepting refusal. But what if refusal causes preventable harm?

Navigating it:
- Ensure the refusal is informed—does the client understand the risks?
- Explore barriers—is something preventing engagement?
- Respect the decision if it's genuinely informed
- Document the refusal and your response
- Maintain the relationship if possible
- Intervene only if there's imminent serious danger

**The Suicidal Client Who Refuses Help**

Among the hardest dilemmas: a client at suicide risk who refuses intervention.

The dilemma: Autonomy says they have the right to make their own decisions. Nonmaleficence says you should prevent serious harm.

Navigating it:
- Assess capacity—is the person able to make informed decisions?
- Suicide often involves impaired decision-making
- Temporary restriction of autonomy may be justified to prevent irreversible harm
- But also consider: will involuntary action destroy the therapeutic relationship and increase risk?
- Use the least restrictive intervention possible
- Consult urgently
- Document thoroughly

**Capacity Questions**

When clients' capacity to make decisions is questionable:

The dilemma: Assuming incapacity violates autonomy. Assuming capacity may allow harm.

Navigating it:
- Capacity exists on a spectrum
- Capacity is decision-specific—someone may lack capacity for some decisions but not others
- Assess capacity carefully rather than assuming
- Seek consultation for complex capacity questions
- Consider guardianship or protective services when appropriate
- Document your assessment

**High-Risk Client Choices**

Clients may make legal but risky choices—staying with an abusive partner, using substances, engaging in risky behavior.

The dilemma: You can see the harm. They have the right to choose.

Navigating it:
- Distinguish between harmful and illegal or imminently dangerous
- Explore the choice—is it informed? What does the client understand?
- Share your concerns without taking control
- Support the client in their autonomy, even when you disagree
- Set appropriate limits on your own participation

### MODULE 6: ETHICS vs. LAW DILEMMAS (20 minutes)

**When Ethics and Law Conflict**

Sometimes ethical obligations and legal requirements point in different directions.

The dilemma: You're obligated to follow the law. You're also obligated to act ethically. What when these conflict?

**Examples of Conflict**

*Immigration status*: You may be ethically obligated to help a client, but certain actions could have legal implications.

*Reporting requirements*: Mandatory reporting laws may require disclosure that harms the therapeutic relationship.

*Court orders*: Courts may order actions you believe are clinically harmful.

*Insurance requirements*: Billing requirements may conflict with clinical judgment.

**Navigating Ethics-Law Conflicts**

Strategies:
- Know both the ethical requirements and legal requirements thoroughly
- Seek legal consultation when facing potential conflicts
- Advocate within legal channels for ethical positions
- If unable to resolve conflict, generally follow the law while documenting ethical concerns
- In extreme cases, consider whether conscientious objection is warranted (with full awareness of consequences)
- Remember that laws can be changed—advocacy is part of ethical practice

**Institutional Policies**

Sometimes institutional policies conflict with ethical practice:

The dilemma: Employer policies may require or prohibit actions that conflict with ethical obligations.

Navigating it:
- Try to understand the rationale for policies
- Advocate internally for policy changes
- Document concerns through appropriate channels
- In serious conflicts, consider whether continued employment is possible
- Do not violate clear ethical standards even if policy requires it

### MODULE 7: CONSULTATION AND SUPPORT (15 minutes)

**The Essential Role of Consultation**

Ethical dilemmas require consultation:
- Multiple perspectives reduce blind spots
- Shared reasoning improves decisions
- Consultation provides support for difficult situations
- Documentation of consultation demonstrates due diligence

**Who to Consult**

*Colleagues*: Peers who understand clinical work.

*Supervisors*: Those with authority and responsibility.

*Ethics committees*: Professional organization ethics resources.

*Legal counsel*: When legal issues are involved.

*State licensing boards*: Some boards offer guidance (though be cautious about self-report).

*Ethics hotlines*: ACA and other organizations offer ethics consultation.

**Effective Consultation**

When consulting:
- Present the dilemma clearly
- Be honest about all relevant factors
- Share your own thinking
- Listen openly to alternative perspectives
- Remember that consultation doesn't transfer responsibility

**Supporting Yourself**

Ethical dilemmas are stressful. Support yourself through:
- Peer support and processing
- Supervision or personal therapy
- Self-compassion—dilemmas are hard
- Learning from difficult situations
- Recognizing that moral distress is normal

### CONCLUSION: FINDING YOUR WAY (10 minutes)

Between a rock and a hard place. That's where ethical dilemmas put us—caught between competing obligations, uncertain about the right path, unable to avoid all harm.

Here's what I want you to take from this course:

**Dilemmas are real.** Don't let anyone tell you ethical practice is always clear. Sometimes reasonable professionals disagree.

**Process matters.** When answers aren't clear, process becomes essential. Use models. Consult. Document. Think carefully.

**Multiple principles operate.** Autonomy, beneficence, nonmaleficence, justice, fidelity—these can conflict. Knowing they conflict is the beginning of navigating wisely.

**Consultation is essential.** You don't have to figure this out alone. Consult early and often.

**Document everything.** In difficult situations, documentation protects you and demonstrates thoughtful practice.

**Accept uncertainty.** Even the best decision-making doesn't guarantee right answers. Do your best, then live with the uncertainty.

**Learn from difficulties.** Each dilemma you navigate builds wisdom for the next.

Being between a rock and a hard place isn't failure—it's the reality of complex ethical practice. Your willingness to engage these dilemmas thoughtfully, to seek guidance, and to act responsibly even in uncertainty is what makes you an ethical practitioner.

Thank you for your commitment to navigating the hard places.
