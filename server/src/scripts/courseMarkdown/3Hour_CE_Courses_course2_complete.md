## COURSE INFORMATION

**Course Title:** Mirror, Mirror: The Journey Through Countertransference and Resistance

**Course Description:** "Mirror, mirror on the wall"—the famous fairy tale question reflects our deepest desire to see ourselves clearly. In the counseling relationship, both counselor and client hold mirrors to each other, revealing hidden aspects of self through the phenomena of transference, countertransference, and resistance. This comprehensive 3-hour course guides practitioners through the intricate landscape of these therapeutic dynamics, transforming what many view as obstacles into powerful tools for healing. Participants will learn to recognize, understand, and therapeutically utilize these universal phenomena while maintaining appropriate boundaries and professional effectiveness.

**Target Audience:** Licensed Professional Counselors, Licensed Mental Health Counselors, Marriage and Family Therapists, Clinical Social Workers, and counselors-in-training.

**Credit Hours:** 3.0 CE Hours (Clinical Practice)

**Course Level:** Intermediate to Advanced

---

## LEARNING OBJECTIVES

Upon successful completion of this course, participants will be able to:

1. **Define** transference, countertransference, and resistance from both classical and contemporary theoretical perspectives.

2. **Identify** personal countertransference patterns through structured self-reflection and recognize their origins in the counselor's own history.

3. **Distinguish** between subjective (counselor-originated) and objective (client-evoked) countertransference responses.

4. **Apply** a systematic framework for using countertransference as clinical data to enhance therapeutic understanding.

5. **Recognize** common manifestations of client resistance and understand their protective functions.

6. **Implement** evidence-based strategies for working with resistance that honor client defenses while promoting growth.

7. **Establish** self-care and consultation practices that support ongoing management of countertransference.

8. **Integrate** awareness of transference and countertransference into case conceptualization and treatment planning.

## NARRATIVE SCRIPT

### INTRODUCTION: INTO THE MIRROR (10 minutes)

Welcome to "Mirror, Mirror: The Journey Through Countertransference and Resistance." The title draws from the famous fairy tale where the queen asks her mirror to reveal truth—"Mirror, mirror on the wall, who's the fairest of them all?" She expects confirmation. Instead, she receives uncomfortable truth.

The counseling relationship is filled with mirrors. Clients mirror aspects of ourselves we may not wish to see. We mirror aspects of significant others from our clients' pasts. Resistance emerges when what the mirror reveals feels too threatening to face.

Sigmund Freud discovered these phenomena over a century ago, but they remain as relevant—and as challenging—today. Every counselor experiences countertransference. Every client exhibits resistance. The question is not whether these dynamics will appear, but whether we will recognize them and use them skillfully.

Over the next three hours, we'll journey into the mirror. We'll explore:
- The theoretical foundations of transference and countertransference
- How to recognize countertransference in yourself
- How to use countertransference as valuable clinical information
- The nature and function of resistance
- Practical strategies for working with resistance
- Self-care practices that support this challenging work

This journey requires courage. Looking honestly at our own reactions—especially the uncomfortable ones—is not easy. But it is essential. Our willingness to examine ourselves directly impacts our effectiveness with clients.

Let's begin.

### MODULE 1: THEORETICAL FOUNDATIONS (30 minutes)

**The Discovery of Transference**

Freud's discovery of transference emerged from clinical necessity. He noticed that patients developed intense feelings toward him—feelings that seemed disproportionate to their actual relationship. A patient might become deeply attached, profoundly angry, or desperately dependent in ways that didn't match reality.

Freud recognized that patients were transferring feelings from past relationships onto him. The analyst became a screen onto which patients projected templates from childhood—particularly from relationships with parents. This "transference neurosis" became central to psychoanalytic treatment.

**Classical Transference**

In classical theory, transference involves the unconscious redirection of feelings from one person to another. Key features include:

*Displacement*: Feelings originally directed at someone else (usually a parent) are displaced onto the therapist.

*Repetition*: The client unconsciously recreates relational patterns from the past.

*Distortion*: The client perceives the therapist through the lens of past experience, distorting current reality.

*Resistance to awareness*: The client is typically unaware that transference is occurring.

Transference can be positive (idealization, affection, trust) or negative (hostility, suspicion, contempt). Both provide valuable information about the client's relational world.

**The Discovery of Countertransference**

Freud initially viewed countertransference as a problem—the analyst's blind spots and unresolved conflicts interfering with treatment. He prescribed self-analysis and emotional neutrality as solutions. The analyst should be a "blank screen" onto which transference could be projected.

This view has evolved dramatically. Contemporary perspectives recognize countertransference as inevitable, ubiquitous, and—when properly understood—clinically valuable.

**The Totalist vs. Classical View**

Two major perspectives on countertransference exist:

*Classical (narrow) view*: Countertransference refers specifically to the therapist's unconscious reactions based on their own unresolved conflicts. It's a problem to be eliminated.

*Totalist (broad) view*: Countertransference includes all of the therapist's emotional reactions to the client—both those stemming from the therapist's issues and those evoked by the client's presentation. It's data to be understood and utilized.

Most contemporary practitioners adopt a totalist perspective, viewing all therapist reactions as potentially meaningful.

**Subjective vs. Objective Countertransference**

A useful distinction differentiates:

*Subjective countertransference*: Reactions originating primarily from the therapist's own psychology—their history, conflicts, vulnerabilities. These reactions would occur with many different clients who trigger similar themes.

*Objective countertransference*: Reactions evoked by the client's presentation—their interpersonal style, unconscious communication, projective identification. These reactions would occur in most therapists working with this particular client.

In reality, most countertransference involves both elements. The client's presentation (objective) interacts with the therapist's psychology (subjective) to produce the reaction.

**Projective Identification**

Projective identification, a concept from object relations theory, helps explain how clients evoke reactions in therapists.

In projective identification:
1. The client unconsciously projects unwanted aspects of self onto the therapist
2. The client then relates to the therapist as if the therapist embodies these projected qualities
3. The therapist, through subtle interpersonal pressure, begins to feel and act in ways consistent with the projection
4. The client can then relate to these aspects externally rather than internally

Example: A client with unconscious rage projects hostility onto the therapist, then interacts in subtly provocative ways that eventually evoke irritation in the therapist. The client then experiences the therapist as the hostile one.

Understanding projective identification helps therapists recognize when their reactions are being evoked by client dynamics rather than arising primarily from their own psychology.

### MODULE 2: RECOGNIZING COUNTERTRANSFERENCE (30 minutes)

**Common Signs of Countertransference**

How do we recognize countertransference in ourselves? Several signals warrant attention:

*Emotional intensity*: Unusually strong emotional reactions—excessive attraction, anger, anxiety, sadness, or boredom with a particular client.

*Preoccupation*: Thinking about the client excessively between sessions—rehearsing conversations, worrying, fantasizing.

*Behavioral changes*: Acting differently with this client than others—running over time, being late, forgetting appointments, self-disclosing more or less than usual.

*Somatic reactions*: Physical responses during sessions—tension, fatigue, headaches, arousal, sleepiness.

*Rescue fantasies*: Excessive desire to save, protect, or fix the client beyond therapeutic bounds.

*Avoidance*: Reluctance to address certain topics, subtly changing subjects, dreading sessions.

*Boundary loosening*: Temptation to extend boundaries—special scheduling, reduced fees, more personal sharing, contact outside sessions.

*Overidentification*: Losing therapeutic objectivity, merging with the client's perspective, difficulty maintaining a separate viewpoint.

**The Countertransference Spectrum**

Countertransference reactions exist on a spectrum from mild to severe:

*Mild*: Subtle reactions that don't significantly affect treatment—momentary irritation, fleeting attraction, passing boredom. Common and easily managed.

*Moderate*: Reactions that begin to influence treatment—avoiding topics, extending sessions, looking forward to sessions more than professionally appropriate. Require attention and possibly consultation.

*Severe*: Reactions that seriously compromise treatment—boundary violations, acting out anger, emotional withdrawal, inappropriate self-disclosure. Require immediate consultation and possibly termination and referral.

**Common Countertransference Themes**

Certain client presentations commonly evoke countertransference:

*Idealization*: When clients idealize us, we may enjoy it too much, become protective of it, or become anxious about disappointing them.

*Devaluation*: When clients criticize or dismiss us, we may become defensive, withdraw, or try too hard to prove our worth.

*Dependency*: Dependent clients may evoke rescue fantasies, resentment at being needed, or anxiety about their fragility.

*Hostility*: Aggressive clients may evoke fear, counter-aggression, submission, or withdrawal.

*Seductiveness*: Seductive clients may evoke attraction, discomfort, avoidance, or reciprocal seductiveness.

*Trauma narratives*: Trauma content may evoke vicarious traumatization, numbness, or over-identification.

*Similarity to self*: Clients who share our background, issues, or identities may evoke over-identification or avoidance of our own pain.

*Similarity to significant others*: Clients who remind us of family members or past relationships may evoke reactions from those relationships.

**Structured Self-Reflection**

Developing countertransference awareness requires ongoing self-reflection. Useful questions include:

- What am I feeling during and after sessions with this client?
- How do I feel about seeing this client on my schedule?
- Am I behaving differently with this client than with others?
- What does this client remind me of from my own life?
- What might this client be trying to evoke in me?
- What am I avoiding discussing with this client?
- What am I trying too hard to accomplish?

Regular reflection—through journaling, supervision, or personal therapy—builds the self-awareness muscle.

### MODULE 3: USING COUNTERTRANSFERENCE CLINICALLY (30 minutes)

**Countertransference as Data**

Contemporary perspectives view countertransference as valuable clinical information. Your reactions are data about the client's interpersonal world.

Heinrich Racker described concordant and complementary countertransference:

*Concordant countertransference*: The therapist identifies with the client's experience—feeling what the client feels. If the client is sad, the therapist feels sad.

*Complementary countertransference*: The therapist identifies with someone in the client's life—feeling what others feel in relationship to the client. If the client makes others feel inadequate, the therapist feels inadequate.

Both types provide information. Concordant countertransference helps us empathically understand the client's experience. Complementary countertransference reveals how the client affects others.

**The Countertransference Utilization Process**

A systematic process for using countertransference:

*Step 1: Notice*
First, simply notice your reaction. Don't judge it, suppress it, or act on it. Just observe. "I notice I'm feeling irritated." "I notice I'm feeling protective." "I notice I'm spacing out."

*Step 2: Contain*
Hold the reaction without acting on it or pushing it away. Stay present with the feeling while maintaining therapeutic functioning.

*Step 3: Reflect*
Consider the meaning. Ask yourself:
- Is this primarily about me (subjective) or evoked by the client (objective)?
- What might this reaction be telling me about the client's interpersonal world?
- Have I felt this way with other clients? What was the pattern?
- What might the client be unconsciously communicating?

*Step 4: Utilize*
Use the understanding to inform intervention. This might mean:
- Adjusting your approach based on new understanding
- Making an empathic statement reflecting what you've learned
- Interpreting a pattern you've observed
- Bringing the observation to supervision

**Example of Countertransference Utilization**

A therapist notices feeling unusually anxious when her client mentions his father. She contains the reaction and continues the session. Later, she reflects:

"This anxiety feels disproportionate. What's happening? I notice the client's voice gets tighter when he mentions his father. His body stiffens. But his words are casual, dismissive. Maybe my anxiety is picking up on his unacknowledged fear. I also had a difficult father—that's contributing too. But the intensity suggests I'm also receiving something from him."

In the next session, she might gently observe: "I notice that when you mention your father, there seems to be some tension that your words don't quite capture. I wonder if there might be more feeling there than you're letting yourself know."

**Cautions About Disclosure**

Using countertransference doesn't mean sharing your reactions directly with clients. Disclosure should be:
- Rare rather than routine
- In service of the client rather than therapist relief
- Carefully considered for potential impact
- Framed in terms of the client's experience rather than your own

The goal is to use your reactions to understand the client better, not to burden the client with your experience.

### MODULE 4: UNDERSTANDING RESISTANCE (30 minutes)

**What Is Resistance?**

Resistance refers to anything that works against the progress of therapy. It can be conscious or unconscious, verbal or behavioral, subtle or obvious.

Freud viewed resistance as opposition to making the unconscious conscious. Contemporary views recognize resistance as a broader phenomenon—any force that interferes with therapeutic progress.

**Resistance as Protection**

Modern perspectives understand resistance as fundamentally protective. Clients don't resist to be difficult. They resist because something feels threatening.

Resistance protects against:
- Unbearable affect that feels overwhelming
- Threatening self-knowledge that challenges identity
- Attachment injuries and fears of intimacy
- Change that feels destabilizing
- Loss of defenses that have provided safety

Understanding resistance as protection transforms our relationship to it. Instead of battling resistance, we can appreciate its function while gently inviting the client toward greater openness.

**Forms of Resistance**

Resistance manifests in many forms:

*Behavioral resistance*:
- Missing or canceling sessions
- Arriving late or leaving early
- Not paying bills
- Not completing homework or assignments
- Substance use before sessions

*Verbal resistance*:
- Changing subjects
- Intellectualizing
- Excessive talking that fills space
- Silence
- Humor that deflects
- "Yes, but..." responses

*Relational resistance*:
- Keeping the therapist at a distance
- Excessive compliance that prevents authentic engagement
- Hostility toward the therapist
- Sexualizing the relationship
- Idealization that prevents reality-testing

*Process resistance*:
- Staying at a surface level
- Forgetting content between sessions
- Not applying insights outside therapy
- Maintaining symptom focus despite deeper work

**Secondary Gain**

Sometimes resistance relates to secondary gain—benefits that maintaining the problem provides. A client with anxiety might resist improvement because:
- The anxiety provides an excuse for avoiding challenges
- Others provide extra care and attention when they're symptomatic
- The identity of "anxious person" feels familiar and safe
- Change would require facing responsibilities they're avoiding

Exploring secondary gain with compassion rather than accusation helps clients understand their ambivalence.

### MODULE 5: WORKING WITH RESISTANCE (35 minutes)

**Principles for Working with Resistance**

Effective work with resistance follows several principles:

*Expect it*: Resistance is normal, not exceptional. Its presence doesn't indicate failure or difficult clienthood.

*Respect it*: Honor the protective function. Resistance exists for reasons, even if those reasons are outside awareness.

*Understand it*: Seek to understand what the resistance is protecting against. What would be threatening about moving forward?

*Work with it, not against it*: Battling resistance typically strengthens it. Paradoxically, accepting resistance often reduces it.

*Use it*: Resistance, like countertransference, provides information about the client's inner world.

**Motivational Interviewing Principles**

Motivational interviewing offers valuable principles for resistance:

*Express empathy*: Deep, accurate empathy reduces defensiveness. Feeling understood creates safety for openness.

*Develop discrepancy*: Help clients see gaps between current behavior and deeper values without creating confrontation.

*Roll with resistance*: Rather than opposing resistance, acknowledge it, explore it, even amplify it paradoxically.

*Support self-efficacy*: People move toward change when they believe change is possible.

**Specific Strategies**

*Naming resistance directly*: "I notice that whenever we approach this topic, we seem to veer away. I wonder what that's about."

*Exploring the protective function*: "Part of you seems reluctant to go there. I wonder if that part is trying to protect you from something. What might that be?"

*Normalizing*: "Most people feel ambivalent about change, even change they want. It makes sense you'd have mixed feelings."

*Paradoxical intervention*: "Maybe we shouldn't push on this yet. Perhaps there are good reasons for keeping this protected right now."

*Exploring secondary gain*: "I wonder if there might be some part of you that has reasons for things staying as they are? Not that you want to suffer, but perhaps there are some benefits mixed in with the costs?"

*Tracking patterns*: "I've noticed this happens each time we approach [topic]. Last week we shifted to talking about work, today we talked about your mother. I wonder if [topic] feels particularly hard to stay with."

**Working with Specific Resistance Patterns**

*The intellectualizer*: Connect thinking to feeling. "That's an insightful analysis. I'm curious what you feel when you think about that."

*The silent client*: Explore the silence without demanding speech. "The silence seems important. I wonder what's happening in the quiet."

*The compliant client*: Question compliance. "You're agreeing readily, but I wonder if there might be another perspective inside that we're not hearing."

*The hostile client*: Don't counter-attack. "You seem really frustrated with me. I'd like to understand what's happening for you."

### MODULE 6: SELF-CARE AND CONSULTATION (15 minutes)

**The Emotional Labor of This Work**

Working with countertransference and resistance is emotionally demanding. We contain our reactions. We absorb projections. We manage our own triggering while staying present for clients. This is significant emotional labor.

Without attention to self-care, we risk:
- Compassion fatigue and burnout
- Acting out countertransference inappropriately
- Becoming cynical or detached
- Personal relationship strain
- Decreased effectiveness

**Essential Self-Care Practices**

*Personal therapy*: Many experienced clinicians maintain their own therapy. It provides space to process countertransference, work on personal issues that affect practice, and model the client role.

*Supervision and consultation*: Regular supervision isn't just for trainees. Ongoing consultation provides perspective, catches blind spots, and offers support.

*Peer support*: Connection with colleagues who understand this work provides validation and perspective.

*Boundaries around work*: Limits on caseload, hours, and availability protect against depletion.

*Personal life investment*: Relationships, activities, and interests outside of work sustain the person doing this demanding work.

**Using Consultation Effectively**

When seeking consultation about countertransference:

- Be honest about your reactions, even uncomfortable ones
- Distinguish between subjective and objective components
- Explore what the reactions might reveal about the client
- Consider whether personal work is indicated
- Develop strategies for using the countertransference clinically

### CONCLUSION: THE MIRROR'S GIFT (10 minutes)

We began with the fairy tale mirror—the mirror that reveals truth whether we want to see it or not. In counseling, we're constantly looking into mirrors.

Countertransference is a mirror. It reflects our own psychology—our histories, our vulnerabilities, our growing edges. It also reflects our clients' relational worlds—how they affect others, what they project, what they need.

Resistance is a mirror. It reflects what feels threatening, what needs protection, what isn't yet safe to approach. Understanding resistance helps us meet clients where they actually are rather than where we wish they were.

Both phenomena, understood deeply, become gifts. Countertransference, properly managed, enriches our understanding of clients. Resistance, properly respected, guides us toward what matters most and what needs the most care.

The journey through these mirrors requires courage, humility, and ongoing self-reflection. It requires us to know ourselves well enough to distinguish our reactions from our clients' needs. It requires us to respect the protective functions of resistance while gently inviting growth.

This is advanced clinical work. You've taken an important step by engaging with this material. Continue the journey through ongoing reflection, supervision, and personal work. The mirrors will keep showing you what you need to see.

Thank you for your commitment to this deep work.
