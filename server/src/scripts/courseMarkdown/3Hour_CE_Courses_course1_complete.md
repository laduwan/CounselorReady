# Course: Stop, Drop, and Roll: A Comprehensive Guide to Crisis Intervention
## 3.0 CE Hours | Clinical Practice

## COURSE INFORMATION

**Course Title:** Stop, Drop, and Roll: A Comprehensive Guide to Crisis Intervention

**Course Description:** Just as "Stop, Drop, and Roll" provides a clear protocol for fire emergencies, this comprehensive 3-hour course equips counselors with systematic, evidence-based protocols for responding to client crises. From suicide risk assessment to safety planning, from de-escalation techniques to post-crisis follow-up, participants will develop the confidence and competence to respond effectively when clients are in their most vulnerable moments. This course integrates the Columbia Suicide Severity Rating Scale (C-SSRS), Stanley-Brown Safety Planning, and trauma-informed crisis response into a cohesive framework for clinical practice.

**Target Audience:** Licensed Professional Counselors, Licensed Mental Health Counselors, Marriage and Family Therapists, Clinical Social Workers, and counselors-in-training.

**Credit Hours:** 3.0 CE Hours (Clinical Practice)

**Course Level:** Intermediate to Advanced

---

## LEARNING OBJECTIVES

Upon successful completion of this course, participants will be able to:

1. **Implement** the "Stop, Drop, and Roll" crisis response framework to systematically assess and respond to client emergencies.

2. **Administer** the Columbia Suicide Severity Rating Scale (C-SSRS) to accurately assess suicide risk severity and determine appropriate levels of care.

3. **Develop** comprehensive safety plans using the Stanley-Brown Safety Planning Intervention model with clients experiencing suicidal ideation.

4. **Apply** evidence-based de-escalation techniques to reduce acute emotional dysregulation during crisis situations.

5. **Differentiate** between crisis levels requiring outpatient management, mobile crisis intervention, and emergency hospitalization.

6. **Integrate** trauma-informed principles throughout all phases of crisis intervention to prevent re-traumatization.

7. **Document** crisis interventions in accordance with legal, ethical, and clinical best practices.

8. **Establish** post-crisis follow-up protocols that reduce risk of subsequent crisis episodes and support client recovery.

## NARRATIVE SCRIPT

### INTRODUCTION: WHY "STOP, DROP, AND ROLL"? (10 minutes)

Welcome to "Stop, Drop, and Roll: A Comprehensive Guide to Crisis Intervention." The title of this course is intentional. When we teach children fire safety, we don't give them a lengthy lecture on combustion theory. We give them a simple, memorable protocol: Stop, Drop, and Roll. In a moment of panic, when flames are present, that simple sequence can save a life.

Crisis intervention in counseling requires the same approach. When a client is in acute distress—suicidal, homicidal, psychotic, or otherwise in danger—we don't have time to flip through textbooks. We need protocols that are so deeply ingrained that they emerge automatically, even when our own anxiety is elevated.

Over the next three hours, we'll build your crisis intervention framework using a parallel structure:

**STOP** - Stop and assess. What is actually happening? What is the nature and severity of this crisis? This phase involves systematic risk assessment using validated tools.

**DROP** - Drop your agenda and be fully present. De-escalate. Stabilize. Connect. This phase involves the interpersonal skills that reduce acute distress.

**ROLL** - Roll out the plan. Safety planning, appropriate level of care, documentation, and follow-up. This phase involves the concrete actions that move from crisis to safety.

By the end of this course, these three phases will be as automatic as Stop, Drop, and Roll is for fire safety.

### MODULE 1: FOUNDATIONS OF CRISIS INTERVENTION (25 minutes)

**Defining Crisis**

A crisis is a time-limited state of psychological disequilibrium in which a person's usual coping mechanisms are overwhelmed. Gerald Caplan's foundational work established that crises are not inherently pathological—they are normal human responses to overwhelming circumstances.

What distinguishes a crisis from general distress?

First, there is a precipitating event or series of events that exceeds the person's capacity to cope. Second, the person experiences significant disruption to their psychological equilibrium. Third, usual problem-solving methods have failed or are unavailable. Fourth, there is a time-limited window during which the person is particularly vulnerable—and particularly open to intervention.

**Types of Crises**

Crises can be categorized in several ways:

*Developmental crises* emerge from expected life transitions—adolescence, marriage, parenthood, retirement, aging. These are predictable but can still overwhelm.

*Situational crises* arise from unexpected events—job loss, divorce, accident, illness, death of a loved one. The key feature is the unexpected nature of the precipitant.

*Existential crises* involve confrontation with fundamental questions of meaning, purpose, mortality, and freedom. These may be triggered by other events but center on existential themes.

*Psychiatric crises* involve acute exacerbation of mental illness—psychotic episodes, severe depression with suicidal ideation, manic episodes, severe anxiety or panic.

**The Crisis State**

During crisis, people experience characteristic changes:

*Cognitive changes* include narrowed attention, difficulty problem-solving, black-and-white thinking, and impaired judgment. The prefrontal cortex is partially offline while the limbic system is hyperactive.

*Emotional changes* include heightened affect, emotional lability, feelings of helplessness and hopelessness, and vulnerability.

*Behavioral changes* may include agitation, withdrawal, impulsivity, or paralysis. Sleep and appetite are typically disrupted.

*Physical changes* include autonomic arousal—elevated heart rate, rapid breathing, muscle tension, and stress hormone release.

Understanding the crisis state helps us recognize that unusual behavior during crisis doesn't necessarily reflect the person's baseline functioning or long-term prognosis.

**Trauma-Informed Crisis Response**

All crisis intervention should be trauma-informed. SAMHSA's framework reminds us of the four Rs: Realize the widespread impact of trauma; Recognize the signs and symptoms; Respond by integrating knowledge into practice; and Resist re-traumatization.

In crisis intervention specifically, trauma-informed practice means:
- Recognizing that the crisis itself may be traumatic
- Understanding that past trauma may influence current crisis response
- Prioritizing safety and choice even in emergency situations
- Avoiding interventions that replicate dynamics of past trauma
- Attending to the counselor's own stress response

### MODULE 2: STOP - SYSTEMATIC RISK ASSESSMENT (35 minutes)

**The STOP Phase**

When crisis emerges, our first task is to stop and assess. This seems obvious, but it's not our natural response. Our natural response is to do something—to fix, to reassure, to take action. But premature action without adequate assessment can be dangerous.

Stopping means:
- Pausing our own anxiety response
- Gathering information systematically
- Using validated assessment tools
- Determining the nature and severity of the crisis

**Suicide Risk Assessment**

Suicide is one of the most common and most serious crises we encounter. Every counselor must be competent in suicide risk assessment.

The Columbia Suicide Severity Rating Scale (C-SSRS) provides a standardized framework. It assesses:

*Suicidal Ideation Severity* on a five-point scale:
1. Wish to be dead - passive thoughts about wanting to be dead or not wanting to be alive anymore
2. Non-specific active suicidal thoughts - general thoughts of wanting to end one's life without thoughts of methods
3. Active suicidal ideation with any methods - thoughts of suicide and has thought of at least one method
4. Active suicidal ideation with some intent to act - thoughts of killing oneself with some intent to act on such thoughts
5. Active suicidal ideation with specific plan and intent - thoughts of killing oneself with details worked out and intent to carry out the plan

*Suicidal Ideation Intensity* including frequency, duration, controllability, deterrents, and reasons for ideation.

*Suicidal Behavior* including actual attempts, interrupted attempts, aborted attempts, and preparatory behaviors.

Asking directly about suicide does not increase risk—this myth has been definitively debunked. Clear, direct questions are essential: "Are you thinking about suicide?" "Are you thinking about killing yourself?" "Do you have a plan?" "Do you have access to means?"

**Risk and Protective Factors**

Assessment extends beyond current ideation to the broader context:

*Risk factors* include:
- Previous suicide attempts (strongest predictor)
- Mental health conditions, especially depression, bipolar disorder, schizophrenia
- Substance use disorders
- Family history of suicide
- Access to lethal means
- Recent losses or life stressors
- Social isolation
- Chronic pain or illness
- History of trauma or abuse

*Protective factors* include:
- Reasons for living
- Social support
- Children in the home
- Religious or spiritual beliefs against suicide
- Positive therapeutic relationship
- Problem-solving skills
- Access to mental health care

**Determining Level of Risk**

Based on assessment, we categorize risk level to guide intervention:

*Low risk*: Passive ideation without plan, intent, or means. Strong protective factors. No history of attempts.

*Moderate risk*: Active ideation with some methods considered but no specific plan or clear intent. Some protective factors present. May have history of attempts.

*High risk*: Active ideation with specific plan, intent, and access to means. Few protective factors. Recent or multiple prior attempts. Acute precipitants.

*Imminent risk*: Expressed intention to act now or very soon. Means available. Preparatory behaviors evident. Requires immediate intervention.

**Assessing Other Crisis Types**

Similar systematic assessment applies to other crises:

*Homicidal ideation*: Who is the target? What is the plan? What is the intent? What is the access to means? What is the history of violence?

*Psychotic crisis*: What is the nature of symptoms? Command hallucinations? Persecutory delusions? Level of disorganization? Insight?

*Acute intoxication*: What substances? What quantity? What is the level of impairment? Risk of overdose or withdrawal?

*Domestic violence crisis*: What is the immediate safety situation? Where is the perpetrator? What resources are available?

### MODULE 3: DROP - DE-ESCALATION AND STABILIZATION (35 minutes)

**The DROP Phase**

Once we've assessed the situation, we drop our agenda and focus entirely on de-escalation and stabilization. This phase is fundamentally relational. The person in crisis needs to experience safety and connection before they can engage in problem-solving.

**The Neurobiology of De-escalation**

Understanding brain function during crisis helps us understand de-escalation. During acute stress, the amygdala is hyperactive, flooding the system with stress hormones. The prefrontal cortex—responsible for rational thought, planning, and impulse control—is partially offline.

De-escalation works by activating the parasympathetic nervous system and gradually bringing the prefrontal cortex back online. This happens through co-regulation—the calming presence of another person.

**Core De-escalation Skills**

*Calm presence*: Your own calm is contagious. Slow your breathing. Lower your voice. Slow your movements. Your nervous system can help regulate theirs.

*Active listening*: Give complete attention. Don't interrupt. Don't problem-solve prematurely. Reflect what you hear. Validate the experience.

*Validation*: Acknowledge the legitimacy of the person's distress without necessarily agreeing with their conclusions. "Given what you're going through, it makes sense that you're feeling overwhelmed."

*Empathic statements*: "This sounds incredibly painful." "I can hear how desperate you're feeling." "You've been carrying so much."

*Non-threatening body language*: Keep your body open. Maintain appropriate distance. Position yourself at the same level. Avoid crossing arms or aggressive stances.

*Simple, clear communication*: Use short sentences. Avoid complex explanations. Offer limited choices. Don't overwhelm with information.

**What NOT to Do**

Certain responses escalate rather than de-escalate:

*Don't argue or debate*: "You shouldn't feel that way" or "That's not true" puts the person on the defensive.

*Don't minimize*: "It's not that bad" or "Other people have it worse" invalidates their experience.

*Don't give false reassurance*: "Everything will be fine" may feel dismissive and inauthentic.

*Don't take control unnecessarily*: Preserve the person's sense of agency when possible.

*Don't match intensity*: If they're yelling, don't yell back. Stay calm but engaged.

**Working with Specific Presentations**

*The highly agitated person*: Focus on safety first. Create space. Speak slowly and softly. Offer water. Name what you observe: "I can see you're very upset right now."

*The shut-down person*: Don't demand eye contact or response. Sit with silence. Offer presence without pressure. "I'm here. I'm not going anywhere."

*The suicidal person*: Express care directly. "I'm glad you told me. I want to help you get through this." Ask about their pain. Ask about their reasons for living.

*The psychotic person*: Don't argue with delusions. Focus on feelings, not content. "That sounds really frightening." Maintain calm and predictability.

### MODULE 4: ROLL - SAFETY PLANNING AND APPROPRIATE CARE (35 minutes)

**The ROLL Phase**

Once the immediate crisis has de-escalated, we roll out the action plan. This involves safety planning, determining appropriate level of care, making referrals, and establishing follow-up.

**The Stanley-Brown Safety Planning Intervention**

The Safety Planning Intervention, developed by Barbara Stanley and Gregory Brown, is an evidence-based, collaborative process. Research shows it reduces suicide attempts and is more effective than no-suicide contracts.

The safety plan has six steps, developed collaboratively with the client:

*Step 1: Warning Signs*
"What thoughts, moods, situations, or behaviors might indicate that a crisis is developing?"
Help the client identify their personal warning signs—the early indicators that they're moving toward crisis.

*Step 2: Internal Coping Strategies*
"What can you do on your own, without contacting anyone, that might help you take your mind off your problems?"
These are activities the person can do alone to self-soothe: exercise, music, journaling, breathing exercises, watching a favorite show.

*Step 3: Social Contacts Who Can Distract*
"Who are people you could contact or places you could go that would help distract you from your problems?"
These are people and places that provide positive distraction—not people to talk about the crisis with, but people who help take the mind off it.

*Step 4: People to Ask for Help*
"Who are people you could tell that you're in crisis and ask for help?"
These are trusted individuals—friends, family, sponsors—who the person could reach out to when in distress.

*Step 5: Professionals and Agencies*
"What mental health professionals or crisis services could you contact?"
Include therapist contact information, crisis lines (988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline), local crisis centers, and emergency services.

*Step 6: Reducing Access to Lethal Means*
"What can we do to make your environment safer?"
Means restriction is one of the most effective suicide prevention strategies. Discuss securing or removing firearms, medications, and other potential means.

**Means Restriction**

Means restriction deserves special attention. Research consistently shows that limiting access to lethal means saves lives. This is because many suicidal crises are time-limited—if means are not readily available during the acute period, the crisis often passes.

Conversations about means restriction should be:
- Direct and non-judgmental
- Collaborative—involve the client in problem-solving
- Specific—who will hold the means? Where will they be stored?
- Followed up—check that the plan was implemented

For firearms specifically, options include: storing with a trusted person, using a gun lock, removing from the home, or storing at a shooting range or gun shop.

**Levels of Care**

Based on risk assessment, we determine appropriate level of care:

*Outpatient management*: For lower-risk situations where the person has adequate support, is willing to engage in safety planning, and can commit to follow-up.

*Intensive outpatient*: For moderate risk situations requiring more frequent contact and support.

*Mobile crisis/Crisis stabilization*: Many communities have mobile crisis teams that can provide in-home assessment and stabilization. Crisis stabilization units offer short-term observation and treatment.

*Inpatient hospitalization*: For high-risk or imminent-risk situations where the person cannot be safely managed at a lower level of care. May be voluntary or involuntary.

**Voluntary vs. Involuntary Hospitalization**

When hospitalization is needed, voluntary admission is always preferable. Work collaboratively with the client to help them see hospitalization as a supportive resource rather than a punishment.

Involuntary hospitalization is appropriate when:
- The person poses imminent danger to self or others
- The person is unable to make informed decisions due to mental illness
- Less restrictive alternatives have been exhausted or are insufficient

Know your jurisdiction's laws regarding involuntary commitment. Understand the procedures, criteria, and your role in the process.

### MODULE 5: DOCUMENTATION AND LEGAL CONSIDERATIONS (25 minutes)

**Documentation Essentials**

Thorough documentation of crisis intervention serves multiple purposes: continuity of care, communication with other providers, legal protection, and quality improvement.

Crisis documentation should include:

*Precipitating events*: What triggered the crisis? When did it begin?

*Client presentation*: Mental status observations, affect, behavior, statements made.

*Risk assessment*: Document the specific assessment conducted, responses to questions, identified risk and protective factors, and determined risk level with rationale.

*Interventions*: What did you do? De-escalation techniques used, safety planning completed, means restriction discussed, resources provided.

*Disposition*: What was the outcome? Level of care determined, referrals made, follow-up scheduled.

*Client response*: How did the client respond to interventions? Did they commit to the safety plan? Were they cooperative with recommendations?

*Consultation*: Document any consultation with supervisors, colleagues, or other professionals.

**Legal Considerations**

Crisis intervention involves significant legal considerations:

*Duty to warn/protect*: Under Tarasoff and related case law, counselors may have a duty to protect identifiable potential victims of client violence. Know your state's specific requirements.

*Duty to report*: Suspected child abuse, elder abuse, and in some jurisdictions other situations require mandatory reporting.

*Confidentiality exceptions*: Crisis situations often involve exceptions to confidentiality. Document the basis for any disclosures made.

*Informed consent*: Even in crisis, obtain consent when possible. Document when consent cannot be obtained and why.

*Involuntary commitment*: Understand and follow proper procedures. Document thoroughly.

*Professional liability*: The best protection against liability is competent, well-documented care that follows professional standards.

### MODULE 6: POST-CRISIS FOLLOW-UP AND SELF-CARE (25 minutes)

**The Critical Post-Crisis Period**

The period immediately following a crisis is high-risk. Research shows that the weeks following psychiatric hospitalization are a particularly dangerous time for suicide. Follow-up is not optional—it's essential.

**Caring Contacts**

Brief, caring contacts in the period following crisis reduce suicide risk. These can be:
- Phone calls
- Text messages
- Cards or letters
- Brief check-in appointments

The contact doesn't need to be lengthy or clinical. The message is simple: "I'm thinking of you. I care about how you're doing."

**Follow-up Appointments**

Schedule follow-up appointments before the client leaves your office. Don't leave it to them to call. Research shows that people are much more likely to follow through with scheduled appointments than with instructions to "call and schedule."

The first follow-up should be soon—within 24-72 hours for high-risk situations.

**Continuity of Care**

Ensure smooth transitions between levels of care. When referring to other providers or higher levels of care:
- Communicate directly with receiving providers
- Share relevant assessment information
- Follow up to confirm the client connected with services
- Remain involved as appropriate

**Secondary Trauma and Self-Care**

Crisis intervention is emotionally demanding work. We cannot pour from an empty cup.

*Recognize the impact*: Working with suicidal clients, trauma survivors, and people in acute distress takes a toll. This is normal and expected.

*Immediate processing*: After a crisis intervention, take a moment to breathe, ground yourself, and process the experience.

*Consultation and support*: Debrief with colleagues or supervisors. You don't have to carry this alone.

*Ongoing self-care*: Maintain practices that sustain you—exercise, rest, social connection, activities that bring joy, supervision, and personal therapy if needed.

*Know your limits*: If you're experiencing burnout, compassion fatigue, or secondary trauma, address it. Seek support. Consider adjusting your caseload.

### CONCLUSION: INTEGRATING STOP, DROP, AND ROLL (10 minutes)

We've covered substantial ground in this course. Let's bring it all together.

When crisis emerges:

**STOP** - Stop and assess. Use validated tools like the C-SSRS. Gather information systematically. Determine the nature and severity of the crisis. Don't rush to action without understanding what you're dealing with.

**DROP** - Drop your agenda and focus on de-escalation. Be a calm, regulating presence. Listen deeply. Validate the pain. Use evidence-based de-escalation techniques. Help the person's nervous system settle enough that their prefrontal cortex can come back online.

**ROLL** - Roll out the plan. Develop a collaborative safety plan using the Stanley-Brown model. Determine appropriate level of care. Make necessary referrals. Document thoroughly. Establish follow-up. And take care of yourself.

Crisis intervention is demanding work. It requires knowledge, skill, and emotional stamina. But it is also profoundly meaningful work. In those moments of acute distress, your calm presence, your competent assessment, your collaborative planning—these can make the difference between life and death, between despair and hope.

You now have a framework—Stop, Drop, and Roll—that you can carry with you into every session. When crisis emerges, this framework will guide you. Practice it. Refine it. Let it become as automatic as the childhood fire safety protocol that inspired its name.

Thank you for your commitment to being prepared for these critical moments in your clients' lives.
