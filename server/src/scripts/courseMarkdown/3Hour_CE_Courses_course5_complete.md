# Course: Walking on Eggshells: Strategies for Working with High-Conflict Clients
## 3.0 CE Hours | Clinical Practice

---

## COURSE INFORMATION

**Course Title:** Walking on Eggshells: Strategies for Working with High-Conflict Clients

**Course Description:** The phrase "walking on eggshells" perfectly captures the experience of working with high-conflict clients—the hypervigilance, the fear of saying the wrong thing, the emotional exhaustion. Whether due to personality pathology, trauma histories, or other factors, some clients create intense therapeutic challenges that leave even experienced clinicians feeling deskilled and depleted. This comprehensive 3-hour course provides frameworks for understanding high-conflict presentations, strategies for maintaining therapeutic effectiveness, and essential self-care practices. Participants will learn to work skillfully with challenging clients while preserving their own wellbeing and professional boundaries.

**Target Audience:** Licensed Professional Counselors, Licensed Mental Health Counselors, Marriage and Family Therapists, Clinical Social Workers, and counselors-in-training.

**Credit Hours:** 3.0 CE Hours (Clinical Practice)

**Course Level:** Intermediate to Advanced

---

## LEARNING OBJECTIVES

Upon successful completion of this course, participants will be able to:

1. **Identify** characteristics of high-conflict clients and understand factors contributing to these presentations.

2. **Recognize** common patterns in high-conflict interactions including splitting, projection, and emotional dysregulation.

3. **Implement** validation strategies that de-escalate emotional intensity without reinforcing problematic behaviors.

4. **Establish** clear, consistent boundaries using a compassionate yet firm approach.

5. **Apply** dialectical behavior therapy principles including radical acceptance and wise mind to high-conflict situations.

6. **Manage** personal emotional reactions and countertransference triggered by high-conflict clients.

7. **Develop** treatment frames that maximize therapeutic benefit while minimizing harm and burnout.

8. **Utilize** consultation, supervision, and self-care strategies essential for sustainable work with this population.

## NARRATIVE SCRIPT

### INTRODUCTION: THE EGGSHELL EXPERIENCE (10 minutes)

Welcome to "Walking on Eggshells: Strategies for Working with High-Conflict Clients." If you've ever worked with high-conflict clients, you know the title isn't metaphorical—it captures an actual lived experience.

You know the feeling:
- Dreading the session on your schedule
- Censoring your words, afraid of triggering an explosion
- Feeling like nothing you do is right
- Being idealized one session and devalued the next
- Exhaustion that exceeds what other clients evoke
- Doubting your competence despite years of experience
- Relief when the client cancels—followed by guilt about feeling relieved

High-conflict clients challenge us like no others. They trigger our deepest countertransference. They test our boundaries. They make us question ourselves. And yet, they are often the clients who most need skilled therapeutic intervention.

Over the next three hours, we'll explore:
- Understanding high-conflict presentations
- The patterns that make these interactions difficult
- Validation that works without enabling
- Boundary-setting with compassion
- Managing our own reactions
- Treatment structure that helps
- Staying sustainable in this work

By the end, you'll have concrete strategies for working more effectively with these challenging clients—while taking better care of yourself in the process.

### MODULE 1: UNDERSTANDING HIGH-CONFLICT PRESENTATIONS (30 minutes)

**Defining High-Conflict Clients**

"High-conflict" is a behavioral description, not a diagnosis. High-conflict clients share characteristics regardless of specific diagnosis:

*All-or-nothing thinking*: People and situations are seen as all good or all bad, with little nuance.

*Intense emotional reactions*: Emotions are strong, rapid, and often seem disproportionate to circumstances.

*Blame externalization*: Problems are consistently attributed to others rather than self.

*Preoccupation with others' behavior*: Excessive focus on what others are doing wrong.

*Difficulty taking feedback*: Even gentle feedback is experienced as attack and responded to defensively.

*Pattern of interpersonal conflict*: History of repeated conflicts across relationships and settings.

**Contributing Factors**

High-conflict presentations can result from various factors:

*Personality pathology*: Borderline, narcissistic, and other personality disorders often feature high-conflict patterns.

*Complex trauma*: Early relational trauma can create patterns of hypervigilance, emotional dysregulation, and conflicted attachment.

*Attachment disruption*: Insecure attachment, particularly disorganized attachment, contributes to relational difficulties.

*Neurobiological factors*: Some individuals have nervous systems more prone to emotional reactivity.

*Learned patterns*: High-conflict behavior may have been modeled or reinforced in family of origin.

*Mental health conditions*: Bipolar disorder, PTSD, and other conditions can contribute to high-conflict presentations.

Understanding contributing factors doesn't excuse behavior but informs compassionate treatment.

**Borderline Personality Disorder Patterns**

Borderline personality disorder (BPD) warrants special attention as a common high-conflict presentation. Core features include:

*Affective instability*: Rapid mood shifts, intense emotional reactions, difficulty returning to baseline.

*Interpersonal instability*: Intense, unstable relationships characterized by alternating idealization and devaluation.

*Identity disturbance*: Unstable sense of self, chronic emptiness, identity confusion.

*Impulsivity*: Self-destructive behaviors including self-harm, substance use, reckless behavior.

*Fear of abandonment*: Intense fear of real or perceived abandonment, frantic efforts to avoid being left.

The biosocial model (Linehan) understands BPD as resulting from biological emotional vulnerability combined with invalidating environments—a transaction that makes sense of the presentation.

**Narcissistic Patterns**

Narcissistic presentations create different challenges:

*Grandiosity*: Inflated self-importance, expectation of special treatment.

*Need for admiration*: Excessive need for praise and recognition.

*Lack of empathy*: Difficulty recognizing others' needs and feelings.

*Interpersonal exploitation*: Using others to achieve own goals.

*Fragile self-esteem*: Underneath grandiosity, extreme sensitivity to criticism.

*Entitlement*: Unreasonable expectations of favorable treatment.

The therapeutic challenge: Narcissistic clients may not see themselves as having problems, making engagement difficult.

### MODULE 2: PATTERNS IN HIGH-CONFLICT INTERACTIONS (25 minutes)

**Splitting**

Splitting is the tendency to view people as all good or all bad, with difficulty integrating both qualities:

- The therapist is wonderful and perfect—until they disappoint, and then they're terrible
- Other providers are demonized while the current therapist is idealized (or vice versa)
- Relationships described in extreme terms—people are either saviors or villains

In the therapeutic relationship, you may be idealized initially, then abruptly devalued when you fail to meet unrealistic expectations.

**Projective Identification**

As we discussed in the countertransference course, projective identification involves:
1. The client projects unwanted aspects of self onto the therapist
2. The client relates to the therapist as if these qualities belong to the therapist
3. Through interpersonal pressure, the therapist begins feeling/acting in line with the projection

With high-conflict clients, you might find yourself feeling:
- Angry (their projected rage)
- Helpless (their projected helplessness)
- Incompetent (their projected inadequacy)
- Confused (their projected chaos)

Recognizing projective identification helps you understand that these feelings are data about the client's inner world.

**Testing Behaviors**

High-conflict clients often test:
- Will you abandon them like others have?
- Can you handle their worst feelings?
- Are your boundaries real or breakable?
- Do you really care or are you just doing your job?

Tests include:
- Boundary violations to see if you'll enforce
- Crises to see if you'll respond
- Idealization to see if you'll become grandiose
- Devaluation to see if you'll retaliate
- Demands to see if you'll capitulate

Understanding behavior as testing helps you respond therapeutically rather than reactively.

**Escalation Cycles**

High-conflict interactions often follow predictable cycles:

1. *Trigger*: Something activates emotional vulnerability
2. *Escalation*: Emotional intensity increases rapidly
3. *Peak*: Full emotional dysregulation
4. *De-escalation*: Intensity begins decreasing
5. *Baseline return*: Return to relative equilibrium
6. *Potential repair*: Opportunity for processing and relationship repair

Understanding cycles helps you intervene earlier and navigate peaks more skillfully.

### MODULE 3: VALIDATION THAT WORKS (30 minutes)

**The Validation Paradox**

High-conflict clients desperately need validation—their histories often include profound invalidation. Yet they may seek validation for problematic behaviors, putting therapists in a bind:

- Validating the emotion risks reinforcing the problematic behavior
- Invalidating the behavior risks rupturing the relationship

The solution: Validate the valid, which is the emotional experience, without validating the invalid, which is the problematic response.

**Linehan's Levels of Validation**

Marsha Linehan identified six levels of validation:

*Level 1 - Being Present*: Simply being attentive and engaged. Listening. Not multitasking or appearing distracted.

*Level 2 - Accurate Reflection*: Reflecting back what the client communicates without adding interpretation. "You're saying that when he said that, you felt dismissed."

*Level 3 - Articulating the Unverbalized*: Putting into words what the client hasn't said but seems to be experiencing. "I imagine that felt like a betrayal."

*Level 4 - Validating in Terms of Past*: Understanding current responses based on history. "Given what you experienced as a child, it makes sense you'd react strongly to feeling criticized."

*Level 5 - Validating in Terms of Present*: Acknowledging that responses make sense given current context. "Anyone would feel frustrated if they weren't being heard."

*Level 6 - Radical Genuineness*: Treating the client as a capable person, not a fragile patient. Authentic engagement.

**Validation vs. Agreement**

Critical distinction: Validation isn't agreement.

"I understand why you're angry" doesn't mean "You're right to be angry."
"It makes sense you'd feel hurt" doesn't mean "They did something wrong."
"Given your history, that reaction is understandable" doesn't mean "That reaction was appropriate."

You can validate the emotion while maintaining a different view of the situation.

**Validation and Behavior**

Validating emotions while addressing behavior:

"I can see you're really angry right now—that anger makes sense given how dismissed you felt. And I need you to lower your voice so we can continue talking."

"I understand the urge to reach out to him repeatedly when you're panicking—that fear of abandonment is excruciating. And we've talked about how that behavior pushes him away."

The "and" connects validation to behavioral guidance without negating either.

### MODULE 4: BOUNDARIES WITH COMPASSION (30 minutes)

**Why Boundaries Are Essential**

With high-conflict clients, boundaries are particularly important:

*Client safety*: Boundaries provide structure that contains chaos and reduces harmful behavior.

*Therapist sustainability*: Without boundaries, burnout is inevitable.

*Modeling*: Consistent boundaries model healthy relationships.

*Treatment frame*: Boundaries define the space within which therapeutic work happens.

*Reality testing*: Boundaries provide reality the client needs even when they resist.

**Setting Boundaries**

Effective boundary-setting:

*Be clear*: State boundaries explicitly, not implied.

*Be kind*: Deliver boundaries with compassion, not punishment.

*Be consistent*: Enforce boundaries reliably.

*Be prepared for pushback*: High-conflict clients will test boundaries.

Language examples:
- "I care about you, and I can't be available outside our scheduled sessions except in true emergencies."
- "I want to help you, and that help works best when we stay in our 50-minute sessions."
- "I understand you're hurting, and calling me after hours doesn't actually help you develop the skills you need."

**When Boundaries Are Tested**

When clients test boundaries:

*Acknowledge the underlying need*: "I hear that you're really struggling and want more support."

*Maintain the boundary*: "And our agreement is that we meet once weekly."

*Redirect to skills*: "What we've been working on is building your capacity to manage between sessions."

*Avoid JADE*: Don't over-Justify, Argue, Defend, or Explain. State the boundary and hold it.

**Limit-Setting vs. Punishment**

Boundaries aren't punishment—they're structure:

*Punishment*: "Since you called me three times this week, I'm not going to see you next week."

*Limit-setting*: "We've agreed that calls between sessions are for safety emergencies only. Let's talk about what was happening that led to those calls and how you might handle it differently."

The goal is to maintain structure while exploring what happened and building skills.

**Crisis and Boundaries**

Real emergencies require flexible response. The challenge is distinguishing:
- True safety emergencies requiring immediate response
- Painful emotional states that feel like emergencies but aren't

This distinction isn't always clear. When in doubt:
- Assess for actual safety risk
- Respond to genuine emergencies
- Process boundary violations after the crisis
- Adjust boundaries if pattern emerges

### MODULE 5: MANAGING YOUR REACTIONS (25 minutes)

**Why This Work Is Hard**

Working with high-conflict clients is genuinely difficult:
- Intense emotions are contagious
- Projective identification puts uncomfortable feelings into you
- Boundaries are constantly tested
- Progress is slow and non-linear
- Nothing you do seems right
- You become a target for displaced anger

Acknowledging the difficulty is important—this isn't just about you needing to be better.

**Recognizing Your Countertransference**

High-conflict clients evoke strong countertransference. Common reactions:

*Rescue fantasies*: Desire to save the client, go beyond boundaries, be the one who finally helps.

*Retaliation impulses*: Urge to punish, withhold, strike back.

*Helplessness*: Feeling inadequate, deskilled, hopeless.

*Dreading sessions*: Relief when client cancels, anxiety when session approaches.

*Over-investment*: Thinking about client constantly, losing objectivity.

*Withdrawal*: Emotional distancing, going through motions without engagement.

**Managing Reactions in Session**

When activated in session:

*Notice*: "I'm having a strong reaction right now."

*Breathe*: Use the pause to regulate your own nervous system.

*Contain*: Hold the reaction without acting on it.

*Stay curious*: "What's happening between us right now?"

*Use wisely*: Your reaction is data—what does it tell you about the client's experience?

**Between Sessions**

Processing after sessions:

*Decompress*: Give yourself time to shift after difficult sessions.

*Consult*: Talk with colleagues, supervisors, or consultants.

*Reflect*: What happened? What was triggered? What can you learn?

*Self-care*: Attend to your own wellbeing.

*Don't overprocess alone*: Rumination without support isn't helpful.

### MODULE 6: TREATMENT STRUCTURE (20 minutes)

**Treatment Frame**

Effective treatment with high-conflict clients requires clear structure:

*Explicit agreements*: Clear agreements about session frequency, length, contact between sessions, crisis protocols.

*Consistent schedule*: Regular, predictable session times.

*Defined goals*: Clear treatment targets.

*Role clarity*: What is your role? What isn't your role?

*Limits*: What happens if agreements are violated?

**DBT as a Model**

Dialectical Behavior Therapy offers a comprehensive treatment structure:

*Multiple modes*: Individual therapy, skills group, phone coaching, consultation team.

*Hierarchy of targets*: Life-threatening behaviors first, then therapy-interfering behaviors, then quality of life.

*Structured sessions*: Diary card review, homework review, skill building.

*Team consultation*: Required support for therapists.

Even without full DBT implementation, these principles inform effective treatment structure.

**When to Refer**

Sometimes, working with high-conflict clients isn't appropriate:

*Scope limitations*: The client needs specialized treatment you don't provide.

*Skill limitations*: You lack training or experience for this presentation.

*Countertransference barriers*: Your reactions interfere with effective treatment.

*Client welfare*: The client would be better served elsewhere.

Referring isn't failure—it's appropriate care.

### MODULE 7: STAYING SUSTAINABLE (10 minutes)

**Self-Care Is Essential**

Working with high-conflict clients requires exceptional self-care:

*Physical care*: Sleep, nutrition, exercise—the basics matter more when work is demanding.

*Emotional processing*: Therapy, supervision, consultation, peer support.

*Caseload balance*: Not every client can be high-conflict; balance your caseload.

*Boundaries around work*: Don't take work home in your head every night.

*Regular assessment*: Notice when you're depleting and adjust.

**Consultation and Supervision**

These clients require support systems:

*Regular consultation*: Not just when crises arise, but ongoing.

*Supervision*: Even experienced clinicians benefit from supervision with difficult cases.

*Peer support*: Colleagues who understand this work.

*Team approaches*: When possible, work in teams rather than isolation.

**Burnout Prevention**

Watch for burnout signs:
- Emotional exhaustion
- Depersonalization (treating clients as objects)
- Reduced sense of accomplishment
- Cynicism
- Physical symptoms

Respond to early signs before full burnout develops.

### CONCLUSION: COMPASSION FOR ALL (10 minutes)

We've covered substantial ground. Let me leave you with some core messages:

**These clients are suffering.** Underneath the difficult behavior is genuine pain. High-conflict patterns typically developed as survival strategies in impossible circumstances.

**You can be compassionate AND have limits.** Compassion doesn't mean tolerating everything. Clear boundaries delivered with care are more compassionate than resentful accommodation.

**Your reactions are data.** What you feel in the room tells you something about the client's experience. Use it wisely.

**Structure helps everyone.** Clear treatment frames benefit clients who need containment and therapists who need sustainability.

**You can't do this alone.** Consultation, supervision, and peer support aren't optional—they're essential.

**Take care of yourself.** You can't pour from an empty cup. Your wellbeing matters—not just for you, but for your clients.

High-conflict clients are challenging. They're also often the clients who most need skilled, compassionate intervention. By developing your capacity to work skillfully with these presentations, you expand access to care for people who desperately need it.

Thank you for your commitment to this difficult, important work.
