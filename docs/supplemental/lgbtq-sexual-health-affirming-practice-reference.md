# LGBTQ+ Sexual Health: Affirming Practice

## Key Frameworks

**Meyer's Minority Stress Model (2003)** — The foundational framework for understanding elevated mental health risks in LGBTQ+ populations. Distinguishes distal stressors (objective external events: discrimination, violence, legal exclusion) from proximal stressors (internalized processes: stigma, concealment, anticipation of rejection). Chronic minority stress — not identity itself — drives higher rates of depression, anxiety, substance use, and suicidality. Resilience factors include community connection, identity integration, chosen family support, and cultural affirmation.

**Affirming Counseling Framework** — Positions LGBTQ+ identities as normal human variation requiring no explanation or justification. Therapeutic work targets the effects of minority stress and societal stigma, not the identity. Active components: use of correct name/pronouns, non-assumptive intake questions, exploration of identity within cultural context, and addressing internalized homophobia/transphobia as a clinical concern when present.

**Intersectionality** — LGBTQ+ clients exist within multiple marginalized identities simultaneously. Race, ethnicity, religion, disability, immigration status, and socioeconomic background amplify or buffer minority stress. A Black gay man navigating evangelical family systems experiences qualitatively different stressors than a white gay man in an affirming urban environment. Treatment must be individualized accordingly.

## Clinical Quick Reference

**Sexual Orientation vs. Gender Identity**
- Sexual orientation: pattern of romantic/emotional/sexual attraction (gay, lesbian, bisexual, pansexual, asexual, etc.)
- Gender identity: internal sense of one's own gender (man, woman, nonbinary, genderqueer, etc.)
- These are independent dimensions — never conflate them. Transgender people hold the full range of sexual orientations.
- Behavior, identity, and attraction do not always align; assess each separately in intake.

**Coming Out Across the Lifespan**
- Coming out is an ongoing, context-dependent process, not a single event — clients re-come-out in each new relationship, job, and family context
- Adolescents: family rejection is the primary predictor of homelessness, suicidality, and substance use; family inclusion work is highest-priority intervention when feasible
- Older adults: coming out after decades may involve grief over time lost to closeted living; validate without minimizing
- Bicultural clients: may face conflict between cultural/religious identity and LGBTQ+ identity; avoid triangulating these; hold complexity

**Conversion Therapy — Absolute Contraindication**
Conversion or reparative therapy is contraindicated by APA, ACA, NASW, AMHCA, and CACREP. Documented outcomes include increased depression, anxiety, self-harm, and suicidality with no evidence of efficacy. Many states prohibit its use with minors. This prohibition applies to all modalities: individual therapy, group, religious-framed, or "exploratory" frameworks that pathologize identity.

**WPATH Standards of Care, 8th Edition (2022)**
- Removed blanket age minimums for some gender-affirming interventions
- Endorsed informed consent model over diagnostic gatekeeping for adults
- Mental health letters remain appropriate for surgical referrals but should be supportive assessments, not gatekeeping hurdles
- Counselors without specific gender care training should refer rather than issue letters for gender-affirming surgeries

## Common Presentations and Affirming Responses

| Presentation | Affirming Clinical Response |
|---|---|
| Identity confusion / questioning | Provide space without pressure to label; psychoeducation about identity fluidity; explore minority stress |
| Internalized homophobia/transphobia | Name it without shame; explore origin in family/religious/cultural messaging; gradual identity integration work |
| Depression/anxiety with no clear trigger | Screen for minority stress, concealment, isolation from community; connect to LGBTQ+ affirming support |
| Relationship issues in same-sex or queer couples | Apply same couples frameworks; do not assume relationship structure mirrors heterosexual norms |
| Trans client seeking support through gender transition | Affirm identity; address minority stress; support social/medical decisions as client-led; refer for medical consultation as needed |
| Adolescent with rejecting family | Safety assessment; explore family engagement; know local LGBTQ+ youth housing resources |

**PrEP / HIV Prevention in Clinical Context**
PrEP (pre-exposure prophylaxis, daily oral Truvada/Descovy or injectable cabotegravir) reduces HIV transmission risk by approximately 99% with adherence. Counselors should normalize discussion of PrEP, reduce stigma, and refer clients to PCP or infectious disease for prescribing. PEP (post-exposure prophylaxis) must begin within 72 hours of exposure — know local prescribers. For clients living with HIV on antiretroviral therapy (ART): Undetectable = Untransmittable (U=U) is scientifically established; affirm this to reduce shame and stigma.

**Chosen Family**
For many LGBTQ+ clients, especially those with rejecting families of origin, chosen family represents primary attachment and support network. Treat chosen family with clinical seriousness equal to biological family. Support its development as a resilience and protective factor.
