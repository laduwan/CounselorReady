# Consent, Power & Boundaries: A Clinical Guide for Mental Health Professionals

## Consent as an Ongoing Process

Consent is not a one-time event but a continuous relational process requiring four elements: capacity, voluntariness, information, and affirmative expression. Clinicians should assess whether clients can freely give or withdraw consent in their intimate relationships. The FRIES framework (Freely given, Reversible, Informed, Enthusiastic, Specific) provides a practical structure for psychoeducation. A key clinical task is helping clients distinguish obligation from desire and recognize that silence or compliance under pressure is not consent.

## Coercive Control: Evan Stark's Model

Evan Stark's coercive control framework reconceptualizes intimate partner violence (IPV) as a pattern of liberty deprivation rather than discrete violent incidents. Perpetrators regulate daily life through micro-surveillance, isolation, degradation, and the weaponization of intimate knowledge. In the sexual domain, coercive control manifests as monitoring reproductive choices, using sexual compliance as proof of love or loyalty, and creating conditions where refusal feels unsafe or impossible. Clinicians often encounter coercive control before clients identify violence; ask about decision-making autonomy, not only physical injury.

## Sexual Coercion Continuum

Sexual coercion exists along a continuum from persistent pressure and emotional manipulation through verbal threats to physical force. Clients frequently do not label pressure-based experiences as coercion. Non-pejorative, descriptive language is more effective: "Did you feel free to say no?" rather than "Were you coerced?" Assessment should include the client's perception of their ability to refuse, not only the perpetrator's behavior.

## Lethality Assessment

When IPV or sexual violence is disclosed, assess lethality using validated instruments (Danger Assessment, Campbell 2004; Lethality Assessment Program). Key elevating factors include firearm access, strangulation history (increases homicide risk approximately seven-fold), threats of homicide or suicide, recent separation (the highest-risk period), and escalating frequency or severity of violence. Document findings, communicate risk clearly, and develop a safety plan collaboratively with the client.

## Mandatory Reporting Obligations

Mandatory reporting requirements vary by state and must be known before disclosure occurs. Universal obligations include suspected abuse of minors and, in most states, vulnerable adults. When reporting, document the date and time of the report, agency contacted, intake worker name, and information provided. Inform the client of the report when clinically safe. When uncertain, consult with a supervisor, ethics board, or legal counsel — and document that consultation. Reporting is not a finding of guilt; it initiates an investigation.

## Power Differentials in the Therapeutic Relationship

The therapeutic relationship is inherently asymmetrical. Clinicians hold power through expertise, gatekeeping of diagnoses, and social trust. This asymmetry requires ongoing attention to how it shapes disclosure, compliance, and the client's sense of safety to disagree. Distinguish boundary crossings (deviations from standard practice that may be clinically justified) from boundary violations (exploitation of the relationship for the therapist's benefit, which are always unethical).

## Counselor Self-Disclosure Ethics

Self-disclosure is appropriate only when it serves the client's therapeutic goals, is directly relevant to the presenting concern, and does not shift the relational focus from client to therapist. Before disclosing, ask: Is this for them, or for me? Document the rationale in session notes.

## Documentation Best Practices

- Record client's own words in quotation marks when describing abuse, coercion, or boundary violations.
- Use behavioral language rather than diagnostic conclusions when documenting power dynamics.
- Document safety planning completed, resources offered, and the client's response.
- Avoid language implying victim culpability ("admitted," "claims," "alleges without corroboration").
- For mandatory reports, document even when certainty is incomplete — reporting is a legal obligation, not a clinical verdict.
