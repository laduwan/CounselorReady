# Cultural & Religious Influences on Sexual Health

## Cultural Humility vs. Cultural Competence

Cultural competence — acquiring knowledge about specific cultural groups — risks stereotyping and a false sense of mastery. Cultural humility, as articulated by Tervalon and Murray-Garcia (1998), is a lifelong practice of self-reflection, recognition of power imbalances, and deference to the client as the expert on their own identity. In sexual health practice, humility means approaching each client's cultural and religious framework with curiosity rather than a checklist. Anti-essentialism is essential: no individual fully represents their group, and intra-group variation is as significant as inter-group difference.

## Sexuality Across Cultural Frameworks

Sexual norms are culturally constructed and transmitted through family systems, religious institutions, and community structures. What counts as healthy, normal, or transgressive varies widely across cultures and historical periods. Clinicians trained in Western individualist frameworks may default to autonomy-centered sexual values that conflict with collectivist or relational sexual ethics held by many clients. The clinical task is not to import a different framework but to understand the client's framework from the inside.

## Religious Teachings and Clinical Application

Major religious traditions hold diverse and internally contested views on sexuality. Christianity, Islam, Judaism, Hinduism, and Buddhism each contain wide intra-tradition variation. Rather than consulting tradition-level generalizations, clinicians should ask clients directly: "What does your faith say about this, and what do you believe?" Shame-based sexual distress associated with purity culture or religious prohibition is a legitimate clinical target — but the solution is not the client abandoning their faith; it is the reduction of harm within a framework the client chooses.

## Acculturation and Sexual Values

Clients navigating immigration, generational differences, or biculturalism often experience tension between inherited sexual values and those of the dominant culture. Acculturation stress in the sexual domain may present as conflict with family expectations, guilt about sexual decisions made in the new context, or internalized shame. Clinicians should explore the specific nature of the tension — what the client values, what their community expects, and what they want — without assuming that assimilation to mainstream norms is the goal.

## Intersectionality

Sexual health cannot be assessed apart from gender, race, class, ability, immigration status, age, and religion simultaneously. An intersectional lens recognizes that clients occupy multiple positions, some of which confer privilege and some marginalization, and that these positions interact. A Latina evangelical client's experience of sexual shame may be simultaneously shaped by gender socialization, religious teaching, family loyalty, and her position as a first-generation immigrant — no single axis explains the whole.

## Distinguishing Cultural Difference from Clinical Concern

A sexual value or practice that differs from clinician norms is not a clinical concern unless it (a) causes the client significant distress, (b) impairs functioning, or (c) involves harm to self or others. Clinicians must actively examine whether a clinical formulation is responding to client distress or to their own discomfort with cultural difference. When in doubt, supervision and consultation provide an external check.

## Conversion Therapy Prohibition

Attempting to change a client's sexual orientation or gender identity through any means — including religious or culturally framed interventions — is prohibited by APA, ACA, SAMHSA, NASW, and NBCC. Many states additionally prohibit conversion therapy for minors by statute. Clinicians may appropriately support clients exploring identity distress; exploration is not conversion. The line is who defines the therapeutic goal: if the goal is reduction of distress, that is ethical practice; if the goal is changing orientation or gender identity to match external expectations, it is not.

## Working with Religiously Conservative Clients

Avoid positioning religious commitment as the problem requiring clinical resolution unless the client identifies it as such. Work within the client's framework by asking what their tradition says about their wellbeing, dignity, and relationships. Differentiate between religious distress (e.g., scrupulosity, shame spirals) and religious values (sincere, ego-syntonic commitments). When a clinician's values conflict sharply with a client's, consultation and potential referral are appropriate — continuing to work while harboring undisclosed value conflicts compromises the alliance.
