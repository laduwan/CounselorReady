# Compulsive Sexual Behavior & Intimacy Disorders

## Diagnostic Classification

Compulsive Sexual Behavior Disorder (CSBD) was formally recognized in ICD-11 (code 6C72) as an **Impulse Control Disorder**, not a paraphilia or addiction. The ICD-11 criteria require: (1) persistent failure to control intense, repetitive sexual urges or impulses, (2) significant distress or functional impairment, and (3) the behavior is not better explained by another disorder or medication effect.

CSBD was considered for but ultimately **excluded from DSM-5** (2013). Insufficient empirical evidence and concerns about pathologizing normative sexual variation were central to this decision. Clinicians using DSM-5 billing codes should document ICD-11 CSBD criteria in the clinical record and select the most appropriate DSM-5 category (Other Specified Disruptive, Impulse-Control, and Conduct Disorder; or Sexual Disorder NOS) with a rationale note.

## AASECT Position (2016)

The American Association of Sexuality Educators, Counselors and Therapists does not find sufficient empirical evidence to support classifying "sex addiction" as a standalone diagnostic entity. Clinicians should avoid applying addiction framing to sexual behavior without careful assessment, particularly given the risk of pathologizing diverse but consensual sexual expression. Distress, impairment, and loss of control — not frequency or type of behavior — are the clinical anchors.

## Differential Diagnosis

Several conditions can mimic or co-occur with CSBD, and accurate differential diagnosis is essential before initiating treatment:

- **Bipolar I/II Disorder**: Hypersexuality during manic/hypomanic episodes is episodic and tied to mood elevation. It typically resolves during euthymia. Screen with the Mood Disorder Questionnaire (MDQ) and gather longitudinal mood history.
- **OCD**: Sexual obsessions in OCD are ego-dystonic and driven by anxiety, not pleasure. The goal of compulsive behavior is anxiety reduction, not gratification. Use the Y-BOCS and focus on the function of urges.
- **ADHD**: Broad impulsivity across domains (financial, substance, sexual) suggests ADHD as primary. Screen with the ASRS; assess for childhood onset of executive function difficulties.
- **Substance Use Disorder**: Disinhibition under substance influence can create or amplify hypersexual behavior. Assess sober baseline sexual behavior before assigning a primary CSBD diagnosis.
- **Dopamine Agonist Medications**: Impulse control disorders — including hypersexuality — are a known side effect of dopamine agonists used for Parkinson's disease and restless legs syndrome. Always conduct a thorough medication review and consult prescribing clinician.

## Moral Incongruence

Developed by Joshua Grubbs and colleagues, **moral incongruence** describes the gap between a person's sexual behavior and their internalized moral or religious values. Research shows that perceived addiction to pornography is more strongly predicted by moral incongruence than by actual frequency of use. Clients presenting with high distress about sexual behavior — especially pornography use — may be experiencing a values conflict rather than a compulsive behavior disorder.

The **Pornography Problem Scale-Revised** and explicit values exploration help distinguish moral incongruence from CSBD. Treatment for moral incongruence focuses on values clarification and cognitive flexibility rather than behavioral suppression.

## Ego-Dystonic vs. Ego-Syntonic Presentations

**Ego-dystonic** clients experience their sexual behavior as alien or inconsistent with who they are. They typically arrive motivated for change but carry significant shame, depression risk, and sometimes suicidal ideation. Shame-informed treatment is essential from intake forward.

**Ego-syntonic** clients do not experience their behavior as a problem — the presenting issue is typically external consequence (partner ultimatum, job loss, legal problem). Motivational Interviewing is the entry point before any change-focused work begins. Avoid labeling ego-syntonic presentations as denial or resistance.

## Treatment Approaches

**Cognitive Behavioral Therapy with Relapse Prevention** is the most established approach. Core components include trigger identification (HALT framework: Hungry, Angry, Lonely, Tired), behavioral chain analysis, thought records targeting urge-based cognitions, and structured high-risk situation planning.

**Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT)** is particularly well-suited for clients with high shame or moral incongruence. ACT techniques include defusion from shame-based thoughts, urge surfing, values clarification, and committed action toward meaningful intimacy rather than abstinence-focused goals.

**Intimacy and attachment-focused work** addresses the frequently co-occurring pattern of intimacy deficits. Assess attachment style early. Clients with avoidant or disorganized attachment often use sexual behavior to avoid relational vulnerability. Couples therapy is indicated when partner impact is significant; address partner trauma responses (betrayal trauma) as a co-presenting concern.

## Ethics & Documentation

Use ICD-11 CSBD language in clinical documentation rather than "sex addiction" unless a client uses the term self-referentially. Document functional impairment specifically — not behavior frequency. Always assess for mandatory reporting obligations when any described behavior may involve minors. Identify personal values and biases about sexuality before treating CSBD; seek supervision when value conflicts arise.
