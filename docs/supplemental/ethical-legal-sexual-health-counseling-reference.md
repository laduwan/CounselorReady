# Ethical & Legal Issues in Sexual Health Counseling

## Key Frameworks

**ACA Code of Ethics — Core provisions in sexual health practice**:
- **A.4.b (Personal Values)**: Counselors are aware of their own values and avoid imposing them. Sexual orientation, gender identity, consensual non-monogamy, sex work, and kink/BDSM are explicitly outside the range of correctable "problems." Discomfort with a client's sexuality is a supervisory issue, not a clinical one.
- **A.2 (Informed Consent)**: All confidentiality limits must be disclosed before treatment begins, in writing. Sexual health disclosures (STI status, sexual trauma, sexual behavior) are governed by the same confidentiality rules as any other presenting concern.
- **B.1 (Confidentiality)**: Default to confidentiality. Disclosure without consent requires a recognized legal or ethical exception.
- **C.2 (Scope of Practice)**: Only provide services within the boundaries of your training, education, and supervised experience. The PLISSIT model is the standard for calibrating this in sexual health work.

**NBCC Code — Section 3**: Prohibits sexual and romantic interactions with current and former clients. The prohibition extends to supervisees and applies regardless of client initiation.

---

## Ethical Quick Reference

**Values imposition**: Redirecting a client from their stated sexual goals to goals more consistent with your values — including assuming heterosexuality, monogamy, abstinence, or gender-normative expression — is an ethics violation under ACA A.4.b. If you cannot provide affirming care, your ethical obligation is referral, not redirection.

**Dual-role and transference risks**: Sexual health content activates transference and countertransference at higher rates than many other presenting concerns. Ongoing supervision is recommended. Document supervision consultations in which personal reactions are managed.

**Referral ethics**: Refusing to refer when a client's needs exceed your competence is itself an ethics violation (C.2). A warm referral — with explanation of why you are referring and specific resources provided — is part of standard of care.

## Legal Obligations: Mandatory Reporting

### Child Abuse
- Mandatory in all 50 states. Threshold: **reasonable suspicion**, not certainty or proof.
- Reporters acting in good faith have statutory immunity from civil and criminal liability.
- Sexual abuse includes: fondling, penetration, exploitation, exposure, and production/distribution of child sexual abuse material.
- **Do not delay** to consult supervision or conduct your own investigation — report first.
- Document verbatim disclosure, date/time, agency and person contacted, case/confirmation number.

**Delayed disclosure (adult clients)**: If an adult client discloses past childhood abuse, mandatory reporting applies *only* if the alleged perpetrator currently has access to minors. Consult your state statute and supervisor.

### Adult Protective Services (APS)
- Required when a **vulnerable adult** (elder, person with disability) is suspected victim of abuse, neglect, or sexual exploitation.
- Threshold and definitions vary by state — verify your jurisdiction's statute.
- Document identically to child abuse reports.
- Capacity to consent to sexual activity is a critical assessment issue for clients in care settings, experiencing cognitive decline, or under the influence of medications that impair judgment.

## Duty to Warn / Protect

*Tarasoff v. Regents of University of California* (1976) established that therapists have a duty to protect identifiable third parties from serious, imminent harm threatened by a client.

**Three criteria must all be present**:
1. Serious and credible threat of physical harm
2. Identifiable victim(s)
3. Imminence — harm is foreseeable in the near term

**Acceptable protective actions** (vary by state; know your statute):
- Direct warning to the identified victim
- Notification to law enforcement
- Seek involuntary hospitalization/hold
- Intensify treatment and document safety plan

**STI/HIV disclosure**: Duty-to-warn obligations for ongoing STI exposure are not uniform across states. Some states permit (but do not require) counselor disclosure to an identifiable partner when a client refuses to disclose and exposure is ongoing. This is distinct from HIV criminalization statutes. Know your state law before this situation arises.

## Informed Consent: Confidentiality Limits

Every client must understand — in writing, before the first session — the following limits on confidentiality:

1. Mandatory reporting (child abuse; vulnerable adult abuse)
2. Duty to warn/protect (imminent threat to identifiable third party)
3. Danger to self (imminent suicidality; involuntary hospitalization)
4. Court order or subpoena
5. Supervision and consultation (clarify whether de-identified or not)
6. Insurance/third-party billing (what information is shared)
7. Minors: parental access rights — and state-specific exceptions for sexual health services

## When to Report / Refer

**Report immediately**: Reasonable suspicion of current child abuse or neglect; suspected sexual abuse of a vulnerable adult; credible threat of imminent harm to an identifiable person.

**Refer to specialist**: Client needs SS/IT-level sexual health intervention beyond your training; paraphilic disorder assessment; compulsive sexual behavior with psychiatric comorbidity; sexual trauma requiring specialized protocol.

**Refer when values conflict**: If you cannot provide affirming, competent care due to personal values, provide a warm referral. Document that you referred and why.

**Consult before acting**: Novel duty-to-warn scenarios (e.g., STI disclosure requests), ambiguous mandatory reporting situations, or any case where legal and ethical obligations appear to conflict.
