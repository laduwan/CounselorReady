# Couples Counseling for Sexual Dysfunction

## Overview

Sexual dysfunction within intimate relationships is best understood systemically — as an emergent property of the couple's relational dynamics, attachment patterns, communication repertoire, and individual histories. This reference summarizes evidence-based approaches for clinicians working with couples who present with sexual concerns, desire discrepancy, or dysfunction.

## Couples Sexual History Taking

A thorough couples sexual history is the foundation of effective treatment. Clinicians should assess both the relationship history and individual sexual histories, attending to family-of-origin sexual scripts, prior trauma, and the timeline of when concerns emerged relative to relational events. Key domains include: initiation and refusal patterns, satisfaction and frequency over time, physical symptoms and medical contributors, prior attempts at resolution, and the emotional meaning each partner attributes to the difficulty.

Conducting portions of the history individually — particularly trauma screening and questions about undisclosed experiences — before bringing material back into joint sessions protects clients and improves disclosure accuracy.

## Desire Discrepancy: A Relational Frame

Desire discrepancy is among the most common couples sexual presentations. Effective treatment reframes the pattern as relational rather than assigning pathology to either partner:

- The **lower-desire partner** may have responsive rather than spontaneous desire, may be carrying unresolved conflict or resentment, or may be affected by medical or hormonal factors. Pursuit by the higher-desire partner can paradoxically suppress desire further.
- The **higher-desire partner** may be using sex as the primary vehicle for emotional closeness, experiencing rejection through an attachment lens (shame, abandonment), or inadvertently creating pressure that reinforces avoidance.

Clinicians assess both positions without aligning with either.

## Evidence-Based Treatment Modalities

**Gottman Method** applied to sexual concerns uses the Sound Relationship House framework. The clinician helps couples build Love Maps that include each partner's sexual identity and history, strengthens friendship as the foundation for sexual safety, and applies Gottman conflict management tools to sexual gridlock. Sexual concerns are treated as solvable problems (when behavior-based) or perpetual problems requiring dialogue (when rooted in differing values or desires).

**Emotionally Focused Therapy (EFT, Johnson)** understands sexual vulnerability as an attachment bid. Sexual dysfunction is often a protest against disconnection or a withdrawal response to anticipated rejection. Stage 1 de-escalates the negative cycle around sexual conflict. Stage 2 helps each partner access and share the primary emotions (fear, longing, shame) underlying their sexual position, fostering new cycles of vulnerability and responsiveness.

**Sensate focus** (Masters & Johnson, adapted) reduces performance anxiety by temporarily removing intercourse from the sexual repertoire and replacing it with structured, non-goal-directed touch. Couples debrief the experience of touch itself — sensation, safety, presence — rather than outcome. The progressive return of sexual activity is titrated to the couple's tolerance.

**Sexual genogram** mapping illuminates intergenerational sexual scripts. Each partner traces three generations of family messages about bodies, pleasure, gender roles, shame, and secrecy. Common themes — such as sex as obligation, bodies as sources of shame, or silence as the norm — become visible and available for renegotiation.

## Concurrent Individual and Couples Work

Individual concurrent sessions are clinically indicated when one or both partners carry undisclosed trauma, when there is an active affair, when individual shame inhibits disclosure in the couple's presence, or when ambivalence about the relationship itself is present. Clinicians working in this format should clarify confidentiality parameters at the outset, specifying what information from individual sessions can or cannot be brought into couples work.

## Communication Assessment Priorities

Clinicians assess five communication domains: initiation and bid patterns, consent and refusal language, preference disclosure capacity, post-encounter processing, and the couple's ability to hold a meta-conversation about their sexual relationship outside of the sexual context. Couples who can only discuss sex during or immediately after a sexual encounter — rather than in a reflective, collaborative frame — typically need explicit communication skill-building before behavioral interventions will hold.
