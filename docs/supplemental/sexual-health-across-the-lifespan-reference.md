# Sexual Health Across the Lifespan: Assessment and Psychoeducation in Counseling

## Key Frameworks

**PLISSIT Model** (Annon, 1976) — the organizing structure for sexual health intervention:
- **Permission**: Open the topic; signal it is safe to discuss sexuality; reduce shame
- **Limited Information**: Correct myths; provide evidence-based psychoeducation within your training
- **Specific Suggestions**: Targeted behavioral or relational strategies; requires specialized preparation
- **Intensive Therapy**: Sex therapy and structured protocols; refer to AASECT-certified clinician

LPCs, LCSWs, and LMFTs typically practice at P and LI. Moving to SS requires deliberate additional training and supervised experience.

**Biopsychosocial-Spiritual (BPSS) Framework** — assess all four domains before forming a clinical picture:
- Biological: hormones, medications, chronic illness, pain, aging-related changes
- Psychological: trauma history, body image, anxiety/depression, attachment, sexual cognitions
- Social: relationship dynamics, cultural and religious context, partner communication
- Spiritual: client's own sexual ethics, meaning-making, religious guilt or shame

Biological etiologies are frequently missed when psychosocial history is prominent. Always rule out medical contributors.

**Basson Responsive Desire Model** — many clients (especially women) experience desire *after* stimulation begins, not before. Spontaneous desire is not the only normal pattern. Reframe "low libido" diagnoses when initiation cues are simply absent.

## Clinical Quick Reference

**Desire discrepancy**: Difference in desired frequency or type between partners. Neither level is pathological by default. Distinguish from HSDD, which requires personal distress as a criterion. Therapeutic focus: communication, decreasing performance pressure, needs negotiation.

**Medication effects to screen**:
- SSRIs/SNRIs: delayed orgasm, decreased libido, anorgasmia (most common complaint)
- Antipsychotics: hyperprolactinemia → desire and arousal disruption
- Beta-blockers: erectile dysfunction
- Opioids (chronic): hypogonadism via testosterone suppression
- Hormonal contraceptives: variable effects on lubrication and desire

Never advise medication changes. Flag concerns and coordinate with the prescriber.

## Developmental Considerations

**Adolescence (12–18)**: Normative sexual exploration and identity formation. Assess: sexual activity, STI knowledge, consent understanding, and LGBTQ+ identity. Psychoeducation about consent and healthy relationships is protective and within scope. Flag early sexual activity linked to coercion, trauma, or substance use.

**Young Adulthood (19–35)**: Identity consolidation and attachment formation. Common presentations: performance anxiety, sexual trauma processing, relationship conflict. Avoid assumptions about relationship structure (monogamy, ENM, polyamory).

**Middle Adulthood (36–60)**: Perimenopause and menopause bring GSM (genitourinary syndrome), lubrication changes, and desire shifts. Men experience gradual testosterone decline; ED prevalence rises after 40. Chronic illness onset affects body image and function. Relationship transitions (divorce, repartnering, infidelity) are common.

**Older Adulthood (61+)**: Sexuality does not end with aging — actively challenge ageist assumptions. STI rates are rising in the 65+ population; assess risk without assuming abstinence. In care settings, capacity to consent to sexual activity is a clinical and ethical issue. Partner loss and physical disability reshape sexual expression.

## Assessment Tools

| Instrument | Population | What It Measures |
|---|---|---|
| FSFI (Female Sexual Function Index) | Women | Desire, arousal, lubrication, orgasm, satisfaction, pain — 19 items |
| IIEF (International Index of Erectile Function) | Men | ED severity — 15 items |
| SIDI-F (Sexual Interest & Desire Inventory–Female) | Women | HSDD severity |
| Brief Sexual Symptom Checklist (BSSC) | All | Brief screen; identifies domain for referral |
| SFQ28 (Sexual Function Questionnaire) | All | Multidomain comprehensive measure |

Validated tools support, not replace, a thorough clinical interview. Introduce screening instruments with normalizing language.

## When to Refer

- **AASECT-certified sex therapist**: SS/IT-level concerns, penetration disorders (vaginismus/vestibulodynia), paraphilic presentations, sexual trauma requiring specialized protocols (EMDR-sex therapy integration)
- **OB/GYN or Urology**: Physical pain with sexual activity, suspected hormonal etiology, ED without psychological precipitant, GSM symptoms
- **Endocrinology**: Suspected testosterone or estrogen dysregulation
- **Psychiatry/Prescriber**: Medication-induced dysfunction, hypersexual disorder with psychiatric comorbidity, compulsive sexual behavior requiring pharmacological adjunct

Refer rather than exceed your training — the PLISSIT model gives you explicit permission to do so.

## Ethical Obligations

**ACA A.4.b**: Counselors do not impose personal values on clients. Sexual orientation, gender identity, and relationship structure are explicitly covered. If personal values cannot be bracketed, refer — do not redirect the client toward your preferred outcome.

**Scope of practice**: Psychoeducation and P/LI-level work are within general counseling scope. Sex therapy techniques require formal training. Document your rationale for interventions near the SS level.

**Minors**: Developmentally appropriate sexual health information is protective and ethically supported. Consult state law governing minor confidentiality in sexual health contexts — most states allow minors to consent to STI testing and treatment without parental notification.
