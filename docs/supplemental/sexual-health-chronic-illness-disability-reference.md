# Sexual Health with Chronic Illness & Disability

## Overview

Chronic illness and disability consistently affect sexual health, yet clinicians rarely initiate these conversations. The WHO International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health (ICF) provides a biopsychosocial lens for understanding how health conditions interact with personal factors and environmental barriers to shape sexual well-being. The social model of disability reminds practitioners that barriers are often attitudinal and systemic, not simply intrinsic to the person's body.

## Opening the Conversation: BETTER Model

The BETTER model (Mick, Hughes, & Cohen, 2003) provides a structured framework for clinicians who lack confidence in raising sexual health topics:

- **Bring it up** — The clinician initiates; clients rarely self-disclose without permission.
- **Explain** — Normalize why you are asking; sexual health is a legitimate clinical domain.
- **Tell** — Inform clients that resources exist, from adaptive strategies to referrals.
- **Timing** — Acknowledge this is an ongoing conversation, not a one-time assessment.
- **Educate** — Offer accurate, condition-specific information and correct misconceptions.
- **Record** — Document the discussion and any follow-up plan in the clinical record.

## PLISSIT and Ex-PLISSIT Frameworks

The PLISSIT model (Annon, 1976) organizes intervention intensity across four levels: Permission-giving, Limited Information, Specific Suggestions, and Intensive Therapy. Most counselors can competently deliver the first two levels; the latter two often involve specialized referrals.

The Ex-PLISSIT extension (Taylor & Davis, 2006) deepens the model by requiring permission-giving at every level and centering clinician self-reflection. Practitioners are encouraged to examine their own assumptions about disability and sexuality, and to give themselves permission to refer when specialized expertise is needed.

## Condition-Specific Sexual Health Impacts

**Cardiovascular disease** frequently produces fear of exertion during sexual activity, reduced stamina, and medication side effects (particularly antihypertensives contributing to erectile dysfunction). Psychoeducation about cardiac clearance and energy management is often needed before behavioral interventions can succeed.

**Diabetes** affects sexual health through multiple pathways: peripheral neuropathy, vascular changes, and hormonal disruption can produce erectile dysfunction, reduced lubrication, and anorgasmia. Collaborative care with endocrinology and urology or gynecology is indicated.

**Cancer survivorship** involves body image disruption, treatment-induced hormonal changes, and relationship role renegotiation. Many survivors describe ongoing intimacy concerns years after treatment ends. Oncology sexuality programs and survivor-specific couples counseling are evidence-informed approaches.

**Multiple sclerosis** creates variable and often unpredictable sexual effects including fatigue, spasticity, altered sensation, and bladder concerns. Adaptive positioning, energy scheduling, and symptom timing are practical starting points.

**Spinal cord injury** produces level-dependent changes in arousal, sensation, and fertility. Reflex-mediated and psychogenic arousal pathways differ by injury level, and autonomic dysreflexia is a safety consideration. SCI rehabilitation teams typically include sexual health education components.

**Chronic pain** suppresses desire through fatigue, anticipatory avoidance, and opioid effects, while also straining relational dynamics. A pain management-integrated approach that includes couples work and flexible scheduling improves outcomes.

## Disability-Affirming Practice Principles

- Use language the client uses to describe their own body and condition.
- Do not assume disability diminishes sexual interest, identity, or activity.
- Assess partner dynamics, caregiver role overlap, and relational impact separately.
- Sexual assistive devices (positioning aids, vibrators for neuropathy, penile pumps) are clinical tools — introduce them matter-of-factly when appropriate.
- Interprofessional collaboration with physiatry, occupational therapy, urology, and oncology improves sexual health outcomes.

## Contraindications and Scope Notes

Clinicians without specialized sex therapy training should practice within the Permission and Limited Information levels of PLISSIT and refer for Specific Suggestions or Intensive Therapy. Always assess for sexual trauma history, as chronic illness can interact with prior trauma in complex ways.
