Consent, Power & Boundaries
A Clinical Guide for Mental Health Professionals

Clinical Reference Handout — For Licensed Professional Use

Consent as an Ongoing Clinical Process

Consent is not a one-time event. It is a continuous, relational process requiring capacity, voluntariness, information, and affirmative expression. In clinical contexts, therapists must attend to how power differentials may impair the client's perceived freedom to withhold or withdraw consent.

FRIES Consent Framework

  • Freely given — no coercion, pressure, or intoxication
  • Reversible — can be withdrawn at any time
  • Informed — all relevant information provided
  • Enthusiastic — active, not merely absent "no"
  • Specific — agreement to one act ≠ agreement to others

Consent Conversation Framework

1
Open space: "Let's talk about what you're comfortable with."
2
Normalize checking in: "It's always okay to change your mind."
3
Identify barriers: "What makes it hard to say no?"
4
Rehearse language: Role-play boundary-setting scripts.
5
Revisit regularly: Re-check at each session milestone.

Coercive Control — Evan Stark's Framework

Coercive control describes a pattern of behavior that strips autonomy, not just isolated violent incidents. Stark identifies liberty deprivation as the core harm: the perpetrator creates a "micro-regulation" of daily life.

Coercive Control Red Flags (Sexual Domain)

Partner tracks sexual behavior, demands sexual "accounts," or monitors fertility
Uses guilt, silent treatment, or threats to obtain sexual compliance
Controls contraception access or reproductive decisions
Minimizes or denies client's descriptions of sexual coercion ("you wanted it")
Sexual contact occurs during sleep, intoxication, or after prior refusal
Client expresses obligation rather than desire as reason for sexual activity

Sexual Coercion Continuum

  • Pressure tactics: persistent asking, emotional manipulation, sulking
  • Intoxication: using substances to lower resistance
  • Verbal coercion: threats, ultimatums, blackmail
  • Physical force: restraint, assault, weapon threat

Clinical note: Clients may not label pressure-based experiences as "coercion." Psychoeducation using non-pejorative, descriptive language (e.g., "Did you feel free to say no?") is more productive than labeling.

Lethality Assessment Indicators

  • Access to firearms or weapons; recent acquisition
  • Threats of homicide or suicide by partner
  • Escalating frequency or severity of violence
  • Recent separation or attempts to leave (highest-risk period)
  • Strangulation history (elevates homicide risk 7×)
  • Stalking behaviors post-separation
  • Partner's use of intoxicants; job loss; social isolation of victim

Tools: Danger Assessment (Campbell, 2004); Lethality Assessment Program (Maryland model).

Mandatory Reporting Obligations

Know your state statute. Reporting thresholds, covered populations, and timelines vary. The following are general principles only — verify current state law before acting.
  • Report suspected abuse of minors and vulnerable adults per state mandate — no clinical judgment override.
  • Document: date/time of report, agency contacted, intake worker name, information provided.
  • Inform the client you are reporting when clinically safe to do so.
  • Consult with supervisor or ethics board when in doubt; document consultation.

Therapeutic Boundary Maintenance & Power Differentials

Boundary Crossings vs. Violations

  • Crossing: Deviation from standard practice — may be clinically justified with documentation (e.g., brief self-disclosure)
  • Violation: Exploitation of the therapeutic relationship for therapist's benefit — always unethical

Counselor Self-Disclosure Ethics

  • Is it for the client's benefit, or the therapist's relief?
  • Is the topic directly relevant to client's presenting concern?
  • Does it invite reciprocal disclosure that shifts focus?
  • Document rationale in session notes.

Documentation Best Practices

  • Record client's own words (in quotes) when disclosing abuse or coercion.
  • Document behavioral observations, not diagnostic conclusions, when describing power dynamics.
  • Note any safety planning completed, resources offered, and client's response.
  • Avoid terminology that implies victim culpability ("admitted," "claims").
  • For mandatory reports: document even when uncertain — reporting is not a finding of fact.
