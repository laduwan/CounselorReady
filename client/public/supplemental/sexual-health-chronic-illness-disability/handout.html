Sexual Health with Chronic Illness & Disability
Clinical Reference Handout

Disability-Affirming Sexual Health Conversation Guide

Use these prompts to open and sustain person-centered dialogue. Begin with broad permission-giving and follow the client's lead.

Opening Permission Statements

  • "Many people living with [condition] notice changes in their sexual health or intimate relationships. Is that something you'd like to discuss today?"
  • "Sexual health is part of overall well-being, so I like to ask all my clients about it. How has your condition affected that area of your life, if at all?"
  • "There's no right answer here — some people find this very important, others don't. What feels relevant to you?"

Exploring Impact Without Assumption

  • "What does sexual health or intimacy mean to you personally?"
  • "Has your condition or treatment changed things in ways that matter to you?"
  • "Are there barriers — physical, emotional, relational, or situational — that get in the way?"
  • "What, if anything, have you tried? What's helped?"
  • "Who else is part of this for you — a partner, a care provider?"

BETTER Model Reference (Mick et al.)

Step What It Means Sample Language
Bring it up Clinician initiates the conversation; does not wait for the client "I'd like to ask you about sexual health as part of our overall check-in."
Explain Normalize why you're asking; frame sexual health as clinical care "This is a routine part of how I support clients with chronic health conditions."
Tell Tell clients that resources and referrals exist "There are supports available — from medical options to counseling to devices — if any are useful."
Timing Acknowledge this isn't the only opportunity; revisit across sessions "You don't have to address this today. We can return to it whenever you're ready."
Educate Provide accurate, condition-specific information; correct myths "Fatigue and pain can affect desire — that's a real physiological effect, not a personal failing."
Record Document the conversation in clinical notes to ensure continuity Note concerns raised, resources offered, and follow-up plan.

PLISSIT / Ex-PLISSIT Framework Quick Guide

Original PLISSIT (Annon, 1976)

  • P — Permission: normalize, validate, give client "permission" to have sexual feelings and concerns
  • LI — Limited Information: targeted psychoeducation on condition-specific sexual effects
  • SS — Specific Suggestions: tailored strategies (positioning, assistive devices, timing around medications)
  • IT — Intensive Therapy: referral for specialized sex therapy or interdisciplinary care

Ex-PLISSIT Extensions (Taylor & Davis, 2006)

  • Extended Permission: Permission is re-offered at every level — not just at entry — and is given for the clinician's own limitations too
  • Reflection: Clinician reflects on biases and assumptions about sexuality and disability throughout care
  • Self-permission: Clinician gives themselves permission not to be the expert at every level; referral is a competent response

Condition-Specific Impact Summary

Condition Common Sexual Health Effects Key Clinical Considerations
Cardiovascular Disease Reduced stamina, fear of exertion, medication-related ED, decreased libido Cardiac clearance for sexual activity; address "fear of the heart"; side-effect management
Diabetes Neuropathy, vaginal dryness, erectile dysfunction, hormonal disruption Glycemic control link; lubricants; referral to endocrinology and urology/gynecology
Cancer Survivorship Body image changes, treatment-induced menopause, fatigue, relationship role shifts Oncology sexuality specialist referral; survivor-specific intimacy counseling
Multiple Sclerosis Fatigue, spasticity, sensory changes, bladder concerns, cognitive load Energy management strategies; timing of activity; adaptive positioning
Spinal Cord Injury Variable sensation, reflex vs. psychogenic arousal, fertility changes, autonomic dysreflexia risk Level-specific education; SCI rehabilitation team coordination; sexual assistive devices
Chronic Pain Reduced desire, physical limitations, medication effects, relationship strain Pain management integration; couple-focused work; scheduling and pacing strategies
Social Model Reminder: Disability is understood as the interaction between an individual and environmental/attitudinal barriers — not a personal deficit. Clinical care should address both internal experience and systemic barriers (access, attitudes, relational dynamics) that limit sexual well-being.
