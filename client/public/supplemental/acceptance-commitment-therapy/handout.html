ACT Core Processes — Clinical Reference Handout

Acceptance and Commitment Therapy: Core Processes for Clinicians  |  2 CE Hours

Overview: What Is ACT?

Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT, pronounced as one word) is a third-wave behavioral therapy developed by Steven C. Hayes and colleagues. Grounded in Relational Frame Theory (RFT) and functional contextualism, ACT aims to increase psychological flexibility — the capacity to contact the present moment fully and to change or persist in behavior in the service of chosen values. Unlike traditional CBT, ACT does not target the content of thoughts for modification; instead, it changes the client's relationship to their internal experiences.

The ACT Hexaflex: Six Core Processes

The Hexaflex model organizes ACT's six therapeutic processes around the central goal of psychological flexibility. Three processes involve mindfulness and acceptance (the "opening up" side); three involve commitment and behavior change (the "moving forward" side).

Psychological Flexibility vs. Inflexibility

Psychological Inflexibility (Hexaflex Pathology)

  • Experiential avoidance — attempts to suppress or escape unwanted internal states
  • Cognitive fusion — treating thoughts as literal reality; being dominated by verbal rules
  • Dominance of past/future — rumination, worry, limited contact with the present
  • Self-as-content — rigid self-concept ("I am an anxious person")
  • Value neglect — loss of clarity about what matters; living by social compliance or avoidance
  • Inaction or impulsive action — behavioral rigidity or reactivity unconnected to values

Psychological Flexibility (Treatment Targets)

  • Acceptance / Willingness — open to experience without defense
  • Defusion — thoughts observed, not obeyed
  • Present Moment Contact — aware of now, not lost in narrative
  • Self-as-Context — stable observer perspective
  • Values Clarity — knows what gives life meaning
  • Committed Action — persistent, values-driven behavior

Process-by-Process Clinical Detail

1. Acceptance

Acceptance is not passive resignation — it is an active willingness to have an experience. The clinical target is the struggle with internal states, not the states themselves. Therapists assess the cost of avoidance before introducing acceptance work.

Key techniques: Body-scan expansion; noting the secondary suffering caused by fighting feelings; willingness as a dial (0–10).

Caution: Do not introduce acceptance if the client's avoidance protects them from a genuinely unsafe environment (e.g., ongoing abuse). Safety assessment precedes acceptance work.

2. Cognitive Defusion

Defusion disrupts the literal, evaluative function of language. The goal is not to reduce the frequency or believability of thoughts — it is to reduce their behavioral impact. Clients learn to observe thoughts from a distance rather than act from inside them.

Key techniques: Singing the thought; thanking the mind; labeling ("There's the 'I'm a failure' story"); Leaves on a Stream visualization.

Caution: Defusion can feel dismissive if introduced prematurely. Validate the pain of the thought before defusing.

3. Present Moment Awareness

ACT's mindfulness component emphasizes flexible attention — moving in and out of the present as needed, rather than maintaining unbroken focus. This differs from MBSR's sustained-attention model.

Key techniques: Mindful breathing; "What are you noticing right now?"; sensory anchoring; bringing mindfulness into the values discussion itself.

Caution: Clients with dissociation or trauma histories may find grounding exercises activating. Screen and adapt pacing.

4. Self-as-Context

Also called the "Observing Self," this process offers a transcendent sense of "I" from which all experiences can be observed. It is distinct from self-esteem (evaluative) and self-concept (narrative).

Key techniques: Observer exercise (Hayes); Chessboard metaphor (client is the board, not the pieces); deictic framing ("You at age 10 vs. you now — who is observing both?").

Note: This process has overlap with mindfulness-based self-compassion but is philosophically distinct — it targets ontological flexibility, not self-kindness per se.

5. Values

Values in ACT are chosen life directions — ongoing qualities of action — not goals (which can be achieved and crossed off). A client who values "being a loving parent" can act on that value in every interaction; it is never "done."

Key techniques: Values card sort; Tombstone/Eulogy exercise; Valued Living Questionnaire (VLQ-2); distinguishing personal values from socially imposed rules.

Caution: Do not impose values. Explore, clarify, and reflect. Therapist-stated values are not ACT-consistent.

6. Committed Action

Once values are clarified, committed action builds behavioral patterns — using traditional behavioral techniques (exposure, behavioral activation, skills training) within a values-based frame. Goals serve values; barriers are defused, not eliminated.

Key techniques: Values-linked goal setting; exposure hierarchies framed as willingness; behavioral contracts; reviewing action toward or away from values.

Caution: Avoid "goal obsession" — if a client becomes rigid about achieving a goal at the expense of the underlying value, redirect.

ACT vs. Traditional CBT: Key Distinctions

ACT does not try to change the frequency, intensity, or content of thoughts — it changes the client's relationship to those thoughts. This is the foundational difference from cognitive restructuring.
Dimension Traditional CBT ACT
Theory of change Change thought content → change affect/behavior Change relationship to thought → increase flexible action
Target of intervention Distorted or irrational cognitions Experiential avoidance and cognitive fusion
Role of symptoms Symptoms are problems to eliminate Symptoms are contextually understandable; avoidance of them is the problem
Values Implicit; addressed as client preferences Explicit; central treatment target and motivational foundation
Mindfulness Supplemental skill (esp. in CBT-3rd wave) Core therapeutic process integrated throughout
Homework rationale "Practice these skills to reduce symptoms" "Take these steps toward what matters to you"

Evidence Base: Clinical Populations

Strong Evidence (RCTs + Meta-Analyses)

  • Depression: Comparable efficacy to CBT; particularly effective for treatment-resistant and recurrent presentations. ACT reduces experiential avoidance, which mediates depressive relapse.
  • Anxiety Disorders: Social anxiety, GAD, health anxiety — ACT produces durable outcomes by targeting avoidance rather than symptom suppression. Defusion reduces the behavioral impact of anxious thoughts without requiring belief change.
  • Chronic Pain: ACT is a first-line recommended treatment. The model shifts the goal from pain reduction to improved functioning despite pain, targeting pain catastrophizing and activity avoidance.
  • OCD: Defusion and acceptance complement ERP; ACT framing reduces the moral weight clients assign to intrusive thoughts. ACT-enhanced ERP shows superior outcomes to ERP alone in some trials.

Emerging / Promising Evidence

  • Substance use disorders (acceptance of cravings; values-based motivation)
  • PTSD (defusion from trauma narratives; acceptance of trauma-related affect)
  • Borderline Personality Disorder (combined with DBT skills)
  • Eating disorders (defusion from body-image thoughts)
  • Psychosis (reducing distress and behavioral impact of voices, not voices per se)
  • Workplace burnout and clinician self-care
Clinical Note: ACT is transdiagnostic by design. Its utility rests on the degree to which experiential avoidance and cognitive fusion are central to the client's difficulties — not on the DSM diagnosis.

Cautions and Contraindications

  • Active psychosis with command hallucinations: Defusion exercises that increase noticing of internal experiences may be contraindicated until reality-testing is stable.
  • Acute suicidality: Acceptance work must not be used to accept suicidal urges as "just thoughts." Safety planning and crisis protocols take priority. ACT can resume after stabilization.
  • Early trauma processing: Acceptance is not premature exposure. Do not use acceptance exercises to push clients into trauma material before adequate resource-building and stabilization.
  • Low insight or developmental considerations: ACT's verbal/metaphorical nature requires adequate language comprehension. Adapt with concrete examples, pictures, or simplified language for children, ASD presentations, or intellectual disabilities.
  • Cultural considerations: Some values clarification exercises assume Western individualistic frameworks. Explore collectivist values (family, community roles, spiritual obligations) explicitly. Do not treat relational or religious values as fusion.
