Psychopharmacology for Non-Prescribers
Clinical Reference Handout

3 CE Hours  |  NBCC ACEP #7760  |  GA Integrated Therapeutic Perspectives LLC
Scope of Practice Reminder: Counselors do not prescribe, adjust, or discontinue medications. This handout is a clinical literacy tool — use it to monitor client experience, recognize concerns, and communicate effectively with prescribers. Never advise clients about medication changes.

Antidepressants

Selective Serotonin Reuptake Inhibitors (SSRIs)

Mechanism: Block reuptake of serotonin at the presynaptic membrane, increasing serotonin availability in the synapse.

Common Examples: Fluoxetine (Prozac), sertraline (Zoloft), escitalopram (Lexapro), citalopram (Celexa), paroxetine (Paxil), fluvoxamine (Luvox)

Key Side Effects: Sexual dysfunction, nausea, insomnia or sedation, headache, weight changes, emotional blunting, increased anxiety in early weeks. Risk of suicidal ideation in children, adolescents, and young adults (black box warning — first 2 weeks require close monitoring).

Clinical Notes for Counselors: SSRIs typically take 4–6 weeks for full therapeutic effect; clients often report discouragement in the first 2 weeks. Monitor for agitation, activation, or mood shifts early in treatment. Abrupt discontinuation causes discontinuation syndrome (flu-like symptoms, "brain zaps").

Serotonin-Norepinephrine Reuptake Inhibitors (SNRIs)

Mechanism: Block reuptake of both serotonin and norepinephrine, providing dual-action effects useful for depression with pain or anxiety.

Common Examples: Venlafaxine (Effexor), duloxetine (Cymbalta), desvenlafaxine (Pristiq), levomilnacipran (Fetzima)

Key Side Effects: Elevated blood pressure (especially venlafaxine at higher doses), nausea, sweating, sexual dysfunction, constipation. Discontinuation syndrome more pronounced than SSRIs.

Clinical Notes for Counselors: Watch for blood pressure complaints. Clients stopping venlafaxine abruptly may experience severe discontinuation effects — this is not the same as "addiction." Duloxetine is commonly used for pain conditions alongside depression.

Tricyclic Antidepressants (TCAs)

Mechanism: Block reuptake of serotonin and norepinephrine; also have significant antihistamine and anticholinergic effects.

Common Examples: Amitriptyline (Elavil), nortriptyline (Pamelor), imipramine (Tofranil), clomipramine (Anafranil)

Key Side Effects: Sedation, dry mouth, constipation, urinary retention, blurred vision, orthostatic hypotension, cardiac arrhythmia risk, weight gain. Lethal in overdose — high concern with suicidal clients.

Clinical Notes for Counselors: Less commonly prescribed as first-line due to side effect burden. Amitriptyline is frequently used for chronic pain/sleep. Because TCAs are dangerous in overdose, assess access and safety when clients are at elevated risk.

Monoamine Oxidase Inhibitors (MAOIs)

Mechanism: Inhibit monoamine oxidase enzymes, preventing breakdown of serotonin, dopamine, and norepinephrine.

Common Examples: Phenelzine (Nardil), tranylcypromine (Parnate), selegiline (Emsam patch)

Key Side Effects: Hypertensive crisis with tyramine-containing foods (aged cheeses, cured meats, fermented foods), multiple drug interactions including with SSRIs/SNRIs, stimulants, and many OTC cold medications. Dizziness, weight gain, sexual dysfunction.

Clinical Notes for Counselors: MAOIs require strict dietary restrictions. Clients on MAOIs should not receive medications from other prescribers without cross-check. If a client on an MAOI is hospitalized or receives any new medication, flag this to all providers.

Atypical Antidepressants

Common Examples: Bupropion (Wellbutrin/Zyban) — dopamine/norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor, low sexual side effects, lowers seizure threshold; mirtazapine (Remeron) — noradrenergic agent, causes sedation and weight gain, useful in anorexia/insomnia; trazodone — often used off-label for sleep; vortioxetine (Trintellix) — serotonin modulator with cognitive benefits; vilazodone (Viibryd) — SSRI plus partial 5-HT1A agonist.

Clinical Notes for Counselors: Bupropion is contraindicated in eating disorders due to seizure risk. Mirtazapine's sedating/appetite-stimulating properties make it useful in clients with insomnia and poor appetite.

Anxiolytics

Benzodiazepines

Common Examples: Alprazolam (Xanax), lorazepam (Ativan), clonazepam (Klonopin), diazepam (Valium), temazepam (Restoril)

Mechanism: Enhance GABA activity, producing CNS depression, sedation, and anxiolytic effects. Rapid onset makes them effective but misuse-prone.

Key Side Effects: Sedation, cognitive impairment, memory problems, psychomotor slowing, respiratory depression (high risk combined with opioids), rebound anxiety, dependence with regular use as brief as 2–4 weeks.

Dependence Risk: HIGH. Physical dependence can develop quickly; abrupt discontinuation causes potentially life-threatening withdrawal (seizures). Clients should never stop benzodiazepines abruptly.

When to Refer: Client reports taking more than prescribed; using benzodiazepines from other sources; combining with alcohol or opioids; experiencing memory gaps; expressing desire to stop but being unable to.

Buspirone (BuSpar)

Mechanism: Partial agonist at 5-HT1A receptors. Non-addictive; no cross-tolerance with benzodiazepines. Requires 2–4 weeks for anxiolytic effect.

Key Side Effects: Dizziness, nausea, headache. Much lower side effect burden than benzodiazepines.

Clinical Notes for Counselors: Clients switching from benzodiazepines to buspirone may feel it is "not working" because it lacks immediate sedating effect. Psychoeducation about delayed onset is important.

Hydroxyzine (Vistaril/Atarax)

Mechanism: Antihistamine with anxiolytic and sedating properties. No dependence potential.

Key Side Effects: Sedation, dry mouth, drowsiness. Not appropriate for use requiring alertness.

Clinical Notes for Counselors: Commonly used as-needed for acute anxiety or sleep. Increasingly prescribed as an alternative to benzodiazepines for clients with substance use concerns.

Mood Stabilizers

Lithium

Primary Use: Bipolar disorder (manic and depressive episodes), augmentation for depression, suicide risk reduction.

Monitoring Requirements: Serum lithium levels, thyroid function, kidney function (eGFR/creatinine) — typically every 3–6 months when stable. Therapeutic range is narrow (0.6–1.2 mEq/L).

Toxicity Signs (Refer Urgently): Tremor worsening, ataxia, slurred speech, confusion, nausea/vomiting, blurred vision, irregular heartbeat. Toxicity is a medical emergency.

Clinical Notes for Counselors: Dehydration and changes in sodium intake (diet changes, excessive sweating, illness) can raise lithium levels to toxic range. Ask about recent illnesses or dietary changes in clients on lithium.

Valproate (Depakote/Depakene)

Primary Use: Bipolar disorder, seizure disorders, migraine prevention.

Monitoring Requirements: Serum valproate levels, liver function, complete blood count. Teratogenic — counselors should discuss pregnancy planning resources with female clients of reproductive age on valproate.

Key Side Effects: Weight gain, sedation, tremor, hair loss, liver toxicity (rare but serious), polycystic ovary syndrome risk in women.

Lamotrigine (Lamictal)

Primary Use: Bipolar depression (particularly effective for depressive phase), seizure disorders.

Key Concern: Serious skin rashes including Stevens-Johnson syndrome (rare but life-threatening) — most risk during titration. Clients must be instructed to report any new rash immediately.

Clinical Notes for Counselors: Clients sometimes reduce lamotrigine on their own because it lacks a sedating effect they can feel. Adherence monitoring is important. Drug interactions with valproate and oral contraceptives affect dosing.

Antipsychotics

Typical (First-Generation)

Haloperidol (Haldol), chlorpromazine (Thorazine), fluphenazine, perphenazine

EPS Risk: HIGH — akathisia (internal restlessness), dystonia (muscle rigidity/spasm), pseudoparkinsonism, tardive dyskinesia (potentially irreversible involuntary movements)

Less commonly used as first-line; still used in acute settings and depot injections.

Atypical (Second-Generation)

Risperidone (Risperdal), quetiapine (Seroquel), olanzapine (Zyprexa), aripiprazole (Abilify), ziprasidone (Geodon), clozapine (Clozaril), lurasidone (Latuda), paliperidone

EPS Risk: LOWER but significant metabolic effects — weight gain, elevated blood glucose, dyslipidemia, metabolic syndrome

Clozapine: most effective for treatment-resistant schizophrenia; requires weekly/biweekly WBC monitoring due to agranulocytosis risk.

Clinical Notes for Counselors: Watch for involuntary movements (lip smacking, tongue protrusion, repetitive limb movements) that may indicate tardive dyskinesia. Ask about weight and metabolic changes at each session. Nonadherence is common due to side effects — explore medication attitudes openly.

ADHD Medications

Stimulants

Amphetamine-based: Adderall, Vyvanse, Dexedrine

Methylphenidate-based: Ritalin, Concerta, Focalin

Mechanism: Increase dopamine and norepinephrine. Highly effective; Schedule II controlled substances.

Side effects: Decreased appetite, insomnia, elevated heart rate/BP, irritability, rebound effects when wearing off. Potential for misuse.

Non-Stimulants

Atomoxetine (Strattera) — SNRI mechanism, non-scheduled; full effect in 4–6 weeks

Guanfacine (Intuniv) and clonidine (Kapvay) — alpha-2 agonists; often used for ADHD with emotional dysregulation or tic disorders

Side effects: Sedation (guanfacine/clonidine), nausea (atomoxetine), blood pressure changes

Clinical Notes for Counselors: Appetite suppression can compound depression or disordered eating. Medication "wearing off" in the afternoon/evening can cause mood dysregulation — ask clients about time of day when concerns emerge. Diversion of stimulants is a real risk; screen for misuse in school-age and college populations.

Medication-Assisted Treatment (MAT)

Buprenorphine (Suboxone, Sublocade, Subutex)

Mechanism: Partial opioid agonist at mu receptors; ceiling effect limits overdose risk. Naloxone added in sublingual formulations (Suboxone) to deter injection misuse.

Clinical Notes: Buprenorphine IS an opioid — clients may experience stigma from family, employers, and sometimes within 12-step communities. Counselors play a critical role in normalizing MAT. Missed doses or abrupt stops cause withdrawal. Inductions require careful timing (client in mild withdrawal before first dose.

Naltrexone (Vivitrol, ReVia)

Mechanism: Opioid antagonist — blocks euphoric effects of opioids and reduces cravings for alcohol. No abuse potential. Monthly injectable (Vivitrol) improves adherence.

Clinical Notes: Clients must be fully opioid-free before starting naltrexone (minimum 7–10 days for short-acting, longer for methadone) — premature dosing causes precipitated withdrawal. If a client on naltrexone requires opioid analgesia in an emergency, significantly higher doses may be needed; alert medical providers.

Methadone (for OUD)

Mechanism: Full opioid agonist with long half-life; administered daily at licensed opioid treatment programs (OTPs).

Clinical Notes: Clients must attend an OTP clinic daily (initially); this is a significant scheduling burden. QTc prolongation is a cardiac risk — review other medications. Counselors working with methadone-maintained clients should coordinate with OTP counselors and understand that dose adjustments happen at the clinic level only.

Signs a Client May Need a Medication Evaluation

  • Depressive symptoms persisting beyond 4–6 weeks despite therapeutic engagement
  • Anxiety that significantly impairs daily functioning and does not respond to skill-building
  • Sleep disruption so severe it is undermining therapy progress
  • Mood episodes consistent with bipolar disorder (elation, decreased need for sleep, grandiosity, impulsivity)
  • Psychotic symptoms: hallucinations, paranoia, disorganized thinking, ideas of reference
  • OCD symptoms unresponsive to therapy alone (research supports combined SSRI + ERP)
  • Trauma symptoms with significant hyperarousal or hyperreactivity not responding to stabilization
  • Active substance use that appears physiologically driven
  • Suspected ADHD impairing functioning across settings
  • Suicidal ideation with history of impulsivity — may warrant evaluation for pharmacological support

Scope-of-Practice Reminders

Counselors may: observe and document medication-related behaviors; psychoeducate clients about what their prescriber has told them; facilitate communication between client and prescriber; address medication adherence as a clinical issue; and refer clients for medication evaluation.
Counselors may NOT: recommend specific medications; suggest dose adjustments; advise a client to stop or reduce a medication; diagnose conditions that require pharmacological treatment; or contact a prescriber without client consent (except in imminent safety situations).
When in doubt: document what you observed in session, encourage the client to contact their prescriber, and with written consent, reach out directly to share clinical observations.

Communicating Effectively with Prescribers

Prescribers and counselors often have limited time for coordination. To make contact productive:

  • Be specific and behavioral: Instead of "she seems more depressed," say "In the past two sessions, she has had difficulty completing sentences, reported sleeping 12+ hours, and discontinued her exercise routine."
  • Lead with safety concerns: If there are suicidal ideation, self-harm, or acute agitation concerns, state these first.
  • Note medication timeline: "She started the new dose of sertraline 10 days ago, and her anxiety has increased since the change."
  • Share client's own words: "She reported feeling 'numb' and 'like she doesn't care about anything' since the dose increase."
  • Document every contact attempt: Date, method, who you spoke with, and any response. If no response after two attempts, note that in the record.
  • Obtain written consent first. A signed release of information (ROI) naming the specific prescriber is required before any contact.
