Common Psychiatric Medications — Quick Reference

Psychopharmacology for Non-Prescribers  |  3 CE Hours  |  NBCC ACEP #7760
For counselor clinical literacy only. This reference supports observation, documentation, and prescriber communication. It does not authorize counselors to advise clients on medications. Always verify current medication information with the prescribing provider.

Typical Antipsychotics
(1st generation)		 Haloperidol (Haldol), chlorpromazine (Thorazine), fluphenazine, perphenazine, thioridazine Schizophrenia, acute psychosis, severe agitation, Tourette's EPS: akathisia, dystonia, pseudoparkinsonism, tardive dyskinesia (potentially irreversible); sedation; anticholinergic effects Involuntary movements (lip/tongue/limb); subjective restlessness (akathisia often mistaken for anxiety worsening); falls risk; heat sensitivity
Atypical Antipsychotics
(2nd generation)		 Risperidone (Risperdal), quetiapine (Seroquel), olanzapine (Zyprexa), aripiprazole (Abilify), clozapine (Clozaril), lurasidone (Latuda), ziprasidone (Geodon) Schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, depression augmentation, autism-related irritability Metabolic syndrome, weight gain, elevated glucose/lipids, sedation (quetiapine/olanzapine); clozapine: agranulocytosis (requires WBC monitoring); akathisia (esp. aripiprazole, risperidone) Weight/appetite changes; blood glucose concerns (thirst, frequent urination); clozapine — WBC lab adherence; akathisia vs. anxiety; lipid/metabolic labs; sedation affecting daily functioning

Non-Stimulant ADHD Atomoxetine (Strattera), guanfacine (Intuniv), clonidine (Kapvay), viloxazine (Qelbree) ADHD (esp. with tic disorders, emotional dysregulation, or misuse history) Atomoxetine: nausea, suicidal ideation in children (black box); guanfacine/clonidine: sedation, BP drop; avoid abrupt discontinuation Delayed onset (4–6 wks) — manage expectations; sedation; atomoxetine suicidality warning in pediatric clients; blood pressure complaints; adherence
MAT — Opioid Use Disorder Buprenorphine/naloxone (Suboxone), buprenorphine (Sublocade), naltrexone (Vivitrol), methadone (clinic-dispensed) Opioid use disorder, pain (buprenorphine, methadone) Buprenorphine: constipation, sweating, mild sedation; naltrexone: nausea, reduced opioid analgesia; methadone: QTc prolongation, overdose risk at high doses Adherence; stigma from family/social network; missed OTP appointments (methadone); naltrexone — client must be opioid-free before initiating; attitudes toward MAT; overdose risk if naltrexone lapses then client uses opioids (loss of tolerance)

Red Flag Signs — Contact Prescriber Urgently

These presentations require immediate prescriber notification — do not wait for the next scheduled appointment. If client is in acute medical distress, call 911.

  • New or worsening suicidal ideation in first 2 weeks on antidepressant — especially in clients under 25 (black box warning; activation syndrome)
  • Lithium toxicity signs: coarse tremor, ataxia (unsteady gait), slurred speech, confusion, vomiting, blurred vision, irregular heartbeat — medical emergency; call 911
  • Serotonin syndrome indicators: agitation, confusion, rapid heart rate, high blood pressure, dilated pupils, muscle twitching or rigidity, hyperthermia, diaphoresis — medical emergency; especially risk when combining serotonergic agents
  • Neuroleptic Malignant Syndrome (NMS): high fever, severe muscle rigidity, altered consciousness, autonomic instability in a client on antipsychotics — rare but life-threatening; call 911
  • Any new rash on lamotrigine — possible Stevens-Johnson syndrome; do not wait; contact prescriber same day and advise client to seek medical evaluation
  • Respiratory depression in clients on benzodiazepines combined with opioids or alcohol — call 911
  • Manic switch on antidepressant monotherapy — rapid mood elevation, decreased sleep need, grandiosity, impulsivity
  • Severe hypertensive symptoms in client on MAOI who consumed tyramine-containing food or received a contraindicated medication — severe headache, nausea, stiff neck — call 911
  • Agranulocytosis symptoms in client on clozapine: fever, sore throat, mouth sores — contact prescriber immediately; WBC must be checked

Scope-of-Practice Reminder

Counselors play a vital role in integrated care — within clearly defined limits.

Counselors MAY:

  • Observe and document medication-related behaviors and client-reported experiences
  • Provide psychoeducation that reinforces what the prescriber has told the client
  • Facilitate and encourage adherence as a therapeutic issue
  • Explore client's attitudes, ambivalence, and barriers to medication use
  • Contact prescribers with behavioral observations (with signed ROI)
  • Recognize red flags and initiate referral or urgent communication
  • Refer clients for medication evaluation when clinically indicated

Counselors MAY NOT:

  • Recommend specific medications or doses
  • Advise clients to stop, reduce, or adjust medication
  • Diagnose conditions requiring pharmacological treatment
  • Contact prescribers without a current, signed ROI (except imminent safety)
  • Interpret lab results or medication blood levels
  • Provide reassurance that a medication is safe or working as expected
When uncertain: document the observation, encourage the client to contact their prescriber, and consult your supervisor before taking further action.
Drug-Drug Interaction Caution: Many psychiatric medications interact significantly with common OTC medications, supplements (especially St. John's Wort with SSRIs/SNRIs/MAOIs), and substances including alcohol and cannabis. When a client starts a new medication, OTC product, or supplement, encourage them to inform their prescriber and pharmacist. Counselors are not expected to identify all interactions — but knowing to ask is within scope.
