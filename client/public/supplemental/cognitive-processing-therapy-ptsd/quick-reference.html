CPT Quick Reference Card

Cognitive Processing Therapy for PTSD: A Practical Guide  |  Clinician Reference Only

12-Session CPT Protocol Overview

Session Focus Key Assignment
1 Psychoeducation on PTSD; CPT rationale; introduce stuck points; discuss avoidance as maintaining factor Impact Statement: Why did the trauma happen? What does it mean about you, others, and the world?
2 Review Impact Statement; identify initial stuck points; introduce ABC model (A=situation, B=belief, C=emotion) ABC Worksheet daily; begin Stuck Point Log
3 CPT+A only: Written trauma account (most traumatic moment); read aloud in session; process natural emotions vs. stuck-point emotions Read account to self daily; continue ABC Worksheet
4 CPT+A: Re-read account; identify stuck points within narrative; begin Challenging Questions Worksheet. CPT (no account): begin Challenging Questions Challenging Questions Worksheet on 1–2 key stuck points
5 Review Challenging Questions; deepen Socratic dialogue; address self-blame and hindsight bias Challenging Questions on additional stuck points
6 Introduce Patterns of Problematic Thinking; client identifies characteristic patterns; apply to stuck points Challenging Questions + identify patterns on each stuck point
7 Introduce Challenging Beliefs Worksheet (integrates all prior skills); transition from structured questions to integrated cognitive restructuring Challenging Beliefs Worksheet daily
8–9 Safety module: distinguish past threat from present risk; address hypervigilance; examine evidence for over-generalized danger beliefs Challenging Beliefs on Safety-themed stuck points
10 Trust module: self-trust and trust of others; distinguish healthy discernment from paranoid avoidance; betrayal vs. deception Challenging Beliefs on Trust-themed stuck points
11 Power/Control and Esteem modules: address hindsight bias, self-blame, survivor guilt, shame; reinforce appropriate attribution Challenging Beliefs on Power/Control and Esteem stuck points
12 Intimacy module; review all 5 themes; write new Impact Statement; compare to Session 1; relapse prevention plan Final Impact Statement; maintenance plan; self-monitor for future stuck points

Five Stuck Point Themes — Sample Stuck Points & Challenges

Theme Sample Stuck Points Sample Socratic Challenges
Safety "The world is completely dangerous." / "I will never be safe." / "Bad things always happen to me." "What percentage of the time are you actually in danger right now?" / "Are there any places or times you feel safer?" / "Is there a difference between a risk being possible and it being probable?"
Trust "I can't trust anyone." / "My judgment is broken." / "Everyone will eventually betray me." "Can you think of one person you trust, even a little? What makes them different?" / "Is the fact that one person betrayed you evidence that everyone will?" / "What would you need to see to know someone was trustworthy?"
Power / Control "It was my fault." / "I should have stopped it." / "I have no control over what happens to me." "What did you know in the moment that you didn't know afterward?" / "If a friend told you the exact same story, would you blame them?" / "What would have had to be different for you to have stopped it?"
Esteem "I am permanently damaged." / "I deserved it." / "I am worthless because of what happened." "Does what happened to you define your entire worth as a person?" / "If someone did this to you, who is responsible for that action?" / "What qualities about yourself existed before this event?"
Intimacy "No one will want me if they know." / "I can never be close to anyone again." / "Relationships only cause pain." "Have you ever experienced closeness with another person? What was different then?" / "What do you think knowing about your trauma would make someone feel — is it possible they'd feel compassion?" / "Is staying isolated actually keeping you safe, or is it costing you something too?"

Socratic Questioning Stems

Use these to guide collaborative discovery — not to convince or persuade. Always follow the client's own logic before examining it.

  • "What makes you believe that?"
  • "How do you know that's true?"
  • "What's the evidence for and against?"
  • "Is there another way to look at this?"
  • "What would have to be true for that to be accurate?"
  • "Is it possible / probable / certain?"
  • "What's the worst realistic outcome — and could you handle it?"
  • "What would you tell a close friend in this situation?"
  • "Are you judging yourself by a standard you'd apply to others?"
  • "Is this thought about the past, or is it about right now?"
  • "Are you confusing a feeling with a fact?"
  • "What does this belief cost you to hold?"
  • "Has anything happened that doesn't fit this belief?"
  • "What would need to change for you to believe something different?"

Patterns of Problematic Thinking

Pattern Definition Example in PTSD Context
Overgeneralization Drawing broad global conclusions from a single negative event "Since I was assaulted once, I can never be safe anywhere." (Safety)
Mind Reading Assuming you know what others are thinking, usually negatively "Everyone I meet can tell I'm damaged." (Esteem)
Emotional Reasoning Treating a feeling as evidence that something is factually true "I feel guilty, so I must have done something wrong." (Power/Control)
Should Statements Rigid rules that generate shame or guilt when not met "I should have fought back. I should have known better." (Power/Control)
Jumping to Conclusions Assuming a negative outcome without adequate evidence "If I relax my guard, something terrible will happen." (Safety)
Exaggerating / Minimizing Magnifying the significance of negatives or shrinking positives "One bad moment means my entire therapy is failing." (Esteem)
All-or-Nothing Thinking Seeing people, events, or self in extreme, black-and-white categories "Either I trust someone completely or I can't trust them at all." (Trust)
Labeling Attaching a global negative label to self or others based on one behavior "I'm broken." / "He's a monster." (Esteem / Trust)
Personalization Taking excessive personal responsibility for external events "If I hadn't been there, it wouldn't have happened — it's my fault." (Power/Control)

Safety Planning Reminders

  • Conduct a formal safety assessment at Session 1; repeat at any sign of escalation
  • Active SI with plan/intent: address before initiating trauma processing; document risk level and intervention
  • Active domestic violence with ongoing danger: coordinate safety planning; CPT may need to pause until client is safe
  • Know your state's duty-to-warn and duty-to-protect thresholds — they override therapy confidentiality
  • Create a written safety plan collaboratively; review it at least once in the first 3 sessions
  • Provide crisis line numbers (988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, RAINN 1-800-656-4673)

Trauma Disclosure Reminders

  • Clients are NOT required to describe trauma in graphic detail for CPT to work — the written account is optional (CPT vs. CPT+A)
  • Never push for disclosure faster than the client's window of tolerance permits
  • Normalize that reading/hearing the account will provoke distress — frame this as the processing, not a sign of harm
  • Distinguish natural emotions (grief, anger, fear) from stuck-point-driven emotions (guilt, shame, betrayal); only the latter require cognitive restructuring
  • If a client cannot complete an account without dissociating, consider Phase-Oriented treatment or consultation before proceeding
  • Document consent for trauma disclosure work; re-confirm willingness at Session 2/3
Evidence Base: CPT is supported by 30+ randomized controlled trials. Effect sizes for PTSD symptom reduction range from 1.0 to 1.9 (large). CPT performs equivalently to Prolonged Exposure (PE) on most outcome measures (Resick et al., 2002; Foa et al., 2005; STRONG evidence, VA/DoD CPG 2023). Individual and group formats are both supported. Telehealth delivery is supported post-COVID by multiple trials.
