Overview: The CPT Model

Cognitive Processing Therapy (CPT) was developed by Patricia Resick and Monica Schnicke in the early 1990s, originally validated with sexual assault survivors. It is now one of the most extensively researched, first-line treatments for PTSD across trauma populations including combat veterans, survivors of interpersonal violence, refugees, and disaster survivors. CPT is designated as a strongly recommended treatment by the VA/DoD Clinical Practice Guideline for PTSD, the APA, and the International Society for Traumatic Stress Studies (ISTSS).

CPT is a structured, manualized, time-limited cognitive-behavioral therapy typically delivered in 12 sessions (individual or group format). It targets the meaning-making process following trauma — specifically, how individuals interpret the traumatic event and its consequences. The central therapeutic mechanism is identifying and modifying "stuck points": maladaptive cognitions that maintain PTSD symptoms by preventing natural recovery from trauma.

The Cognitive Model of PTSD

CPT is grounded in information-processing theory. Under normal circumstances, new experiences are integrated into existing cognitive schemas (mental frameworks for understanding the world). Trauma disrupts this integration process in two primary ways:

Assimilation

The individual alters the memory of the traumatic event to fit pre-existing beliefs, rather than updating those beliefs. The trauma is essentially "explained away" in ways that do not challenge the person's worldview but do prevent full processing.

Example: "It wasn't really that bad" or "I must have done something to deserve it" — beliefs that distort the trauma memory rather than confronting the event as it happened.

Over-Accommodation

The individual changes their beliefs about the world too radically in response to the trauma, generalizing the danger, helplessness, or threat far beyond the original event.

Example: "No one can ever be trusted" or "I am permanently ruined" — beliefs that reflect an overcorrection that maintains hypervigilance and avoidance.

Both processes prevent the natural emotional processing of trauma. CPT intervenes by helping clients identify and examine these stuck-point beliefs, evaluate them against evidence, and develop more accurate, balanced cognitions that allow for recovery.

Key Term — Stuck Point: A specific, concise thought or belief that is distorted, overgeneralized, or otherwise problematic, and that directly maintains PTSD symptoms. Stuck points are distinct from emotions (though they drive them). They are written as declarative statements, not questions or feelings. Example: "It was my fault" (stuck point), not "I feel guilty" (emotion).

The Five Stuck Point Themes

CPT organizes stuck points into five thematic domains (derived from McCann & Pearlman's constructivist self-development theory). Clinicians use these domains to identify and prioritize stuck points throughout treatment.

Theme Core Disruption Sample Stuck Points
Safety Beliefs about personal safety and the safety of others; hypervigilance to threat "The world is completely dangerous." / "I will never be safe again." / "Bad things are always about to happen."
Trust Beliefs about the reliability and honesty of self and others; betrayal schemas "I can't trust anyone." / "People will always let me down." / "My own judgment cannot be trusted."
Power/Control Beliefs about one's ability to influence outcomes; helplessness and self-blame "I should have done something to stop it." / "I have no control over what happens to me." / "It was my fault."
Esteem Beliefs about self-worth and the worth of others; shame, self-blame, and devaluation "I am permanently damaged." / "I am worthless because of what happened." / "I deserved it."
Intimacy Beliefs about the ability to connect emotionally and sexually with others "I can never be close to anyone again." / "No one would want to be with me if they knew." / "Relationships only cause pain."

CPT vs. CPT+A (With/Without Written Trauma Account)

CPT (Standard)

Does not include a formal written trauma account. Sessions focus entirely on identifying and challenging stuck points through the ABC Worksheet and Challenging Questions/Beliefs worksheets. Research by Resick et al. (2008) found CPT and CPT+A equivalent in outcomes, making the account optional.

Best for: Clients who are particularly avoidant of the trauma narrative, those with limited literacy, or when time is restricted.

CPT+A (With Account)

Includes a written Impact Statement early in treatment (Session 2) and a full written trauma account in Sessions 3–4, read aloud in session. The account facilitates emotional processing of sensory and narrative details and can surface stuck points embedded in the trauma narrative.

Best for: Clients who have not fully confronted the trauma narrative, or where the clinician identifies that narrative avoidance is a primary maintaining factor.

Sessions Focus Key Assignment

The ABC Worksheet

The ABC model is the foundational cognitive framework introduced in Session 2. It helps clients recognize that beliefs (B), not events themselves, drive emotional consequences.

  • A — Antecedent (Activating Event): The situation, trigger, or external event that preceded the emotional reaction.
  • B — Belief (Automatic Thought): The immediate, often automatic interpretation or meaning assigned to the event. This is where stuck points live.
  • C — Consequence (Emotion): The emotional and behavioral result of the belief. Clients rate intensity 0–100.

The ABC model shifts focus from "the event made me feel X" to "my belief about the event leads to feeling X." This is the entry point for cognitive restructuring. Clients complete at least one ABC Worksheet per day between Sessions 2 and 7.

Socratic Questioning in CPT

Rather than directly challenging or disputing a client's stuck points, CPT therapists use Socratic dialogue — a guided questioning method that helps clients discover inconsistencies and alternatives in their own thinking. The goal is collaborative discovery, not persuasion. Effective Socratic questioning in CPT:

  • Is genuinely curious, not leading or rhetorical
  • Follows the client's language and logic before examining it
  • Asks for evidence and specificity ("How do you know?" / "What specifically happened?")
  • Explores alternative explanations without dismissing the client's experience
  • Paces carefully — moving too fast can feel invalidating; moving too slow loses momentum
Therapist stance: "I believe you had that thought/feeling. Now let's look at it together."

Challenging Questions Technique

Introduced in Sessions 4–5, the Challenging Questions Worksheet provides a structured set of prompts that guide clients to examine their stuck points systematically. Core challenging questions include:

  1. What is the evidence for and against this belief?
  2. Is this belief based on facts or feelings?
  3. Are you confusing what is possible with what is probable?
  4. What is the worst that could realistically happen? Could you cope with it?
  5. What would you tell a close friend who had this same thought?
  6. Am I thinking in all-or-nothing terms?
  7. What are the costs and benefits of holding this belief?
  8. What's a more balanced or realistic way to think about this?

Patterns of Problematic Thinking

Session 6 introduces a list of cognitive distortion patterns that commonly appear in PTSD-related stuck points. Clients learn to identify their characteristic patterns, which increases metacognitive awareness and speeds restructuring.

Pattern Definition CPT-Relevant Example
Overgeneralization Drawing broad conclusions from a single event "Since I was hurt once, everyone will hurt me." (Intimacy/Safety)
Mind Reading Assuming knowledge of others' thoughts without evidence "Everyone can tell I'm broken just by looking at me." (Esteem)
Emotional Reasoning Using emotion as evidence for a factual belief "I feel guilty, so I must have done something wrong." (Power/Control)
Should Statements Rigid rules that create shame or guilt when violated "I should have fought back / said no / known better." (Power/Control)
Jumping to Conclusions Assuming a negative outcome without evidence "If I let my guard down, something bad will happen." (Safety)
Exaggerating/Minimizing Magnifying negatives or minimizing positives "One mistake means I'm a complete failure." (Esteem)
Taking Extreme Positions All-or-nothing, black-and-white thinking "Either I can trust someone completely or not at all." (Trust)

Special Considerations

Complex PTSD and Chronic Presentations

Clients with early-onset, prolonged, or repeated interpersonal trauma (e.g., childhood abuse, domestic violence, refugee trauma) may present with more entrenched stuck points, greater shame, more pervasive schema disruption, and more difficulty tolerating the cognitive work. CPT adaptations for complex presentations include:

  • Extending to 16–24 sessions when needed, addressing affect regulation before entering the stuck-point work
  • Spending more sessions on the esteem and intimacy modules, which are often most impacted
  • Collaboratively pacing the written account (CPT+A) to avoid overwhelming dissociation
  • Integrating emotion regulation skills (e.g., grounding, distress tolerance) as adjuncts without abandoning the cognitive model
  • Using the therapeutic relationship as a trust-building experience, particularly for clients with attachment disruptions

Dissociation

Mild to moderate dissociation during sessions is common and manageable within CPT. When dissociation emerges:

  • Use brief grounding techniques (5-4-3-2-1 sensory, feet-on-floor, slow diaphragmatic breathing) to reorient the client to the present
  • Do not push through dissociation into deeper trauma processing — pause, ground, and return
  • Significant dissociative episodes may indicate a need for EMDR or Phase-Oriented treatment before or alongside CPT
  • Document dissociation patterns; they often carry stuck-point content (e.g., "If I feel it, it will destroy me" — an esteem/safety stuck point that can be addressed directly)
Safety Note: Always conduct a safety assessment at the first session and repeat as clinically indicated. Active suicidal ideation with plan or intent, active psychosis, and current domestic violence with safety concerns may require stabilization before initiating CPT.
