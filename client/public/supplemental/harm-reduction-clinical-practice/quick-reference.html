Harm Reduction Quick Reference Card

2 CE Hours  |  Addictions  |  NBCC ACEP #7760

8 Core Principles

1. Acceptance — Acknowledge drug use as a reality, not a moral failure.
2. Pragmatism — Any reduction in harm is meaningful progress.
3. Autonomy — Clients have the right to make informed choices about their own bodies.
4. Non-judgment — Stigma drives people away from care; examine your own bias.
5. Meet clients where they are — Adapt to their stage of change, not a clinical ideal.
6. Incremental goals — Small steps toward safer use are valid treatment goals.
7. Community involvement — People with lived experience are experts and partners.
8. Equity & anti-discrimination — Structural factors (race, poverty, housing) shape use and access; address them.

MI-Consistent Language for Harm Reduction

Instead of (avoid)Say (MI-consistent)
"You need to stop using." "What changes, if any, would feel right to you right now?"
"That's very dangerous behavior." "Tell me more about how that's been going for you."
"You're an addict." "Person-first: 'person who uses drugs' or 'person with a substance use disorder.'"
"Why would you do that?" "Help me understand what that experience is like for you."
"If you really wanted to, you'd quit." "It sounds like change is complicated. What's working against it right now?"
"Clean" / "dirty" (urine screens) "Negative / positive for substances."
"Relapse" (implying failure) "Return to use" or "lapse."
"You're not ready for treatment." "What kind of support would actually feel useful to you?"

Harm Reduction Strategies by Substance

Substance Key Risks Harm Reduction Strategy What Clinicians Can Do
Alcohol Liver disease, withdrawal seizure, injury, dependence, fetal alcohol spectrum Moderation management; designated alcohol-free days; avoid drinking alone or while driving; identify highest-risk contexts Support client-set drinking limits; screen for withdrawal risk (CIWA); provide psychoeducation on liver and CNS effects; refer to SMART Recovery or Moderation Management
Opioids Overdose (especially with fentanyl contamination), respiratory depression, injection-related infection Naloxone access and training; fentanyl test strips; never use alone (call 1-800-484-3731); MAT/MOUD; avoid mixing with benzos or alcohol Prescribe or facilitate naloxone; discuss MOUD options (buprenorphine, methadone, naltrexone) without stigma; provide fentanyl test strips where legal; link to SSP for injection equipment
Stimulants (cocaine, meth) Cardiovascular events, psychosis (meth), overdose (rare but rising), injection and smoking equipment sharing, sexual risk Sterile supplies for injection/smoking; avoid sharing pipes/straws; rest and nutrition support; reduce chemsex contexts Discuss cardiovascular warning signs; refer to SSP for supplies; address sleep hygiene; assess for stimulant use disorder comorbidities (depression, psychosis); no FDA-approved medications — behavioral support is frontline
Cannabis Cannabis use disorder (especially high-potency products), psychosis risk in vulnerable individuals, cognitive effects in adolescents Potency awareness; reduce smoked use or switch to lower-risk delivery; avoid use during pregnancy; delay use in adolescents Psychoeducation on THC concentration variability; screen for cannabis use disorder; monitor for psychiatric effects in at-risk clients; discuss interaction with psychiatric medications
Poly-drug use Synergistic respiratory depression (opioids + benzos/alcohol); hyperthermia (MDMA); stimulant masking opioid sedation Avoid mixing opioids with CNS depressants; use fentanyl test strips; stay cool and hydrated with MDMA; have naloxone available for any opioid-involved use Explicitly discuss drug interaction risks without lecturing; review naloxone even for clients who don't identify as opioid users (fentanyl contamination is broad); safety plan for highest-risk combinations
Benzodiazepines Dependence, dangerous withdrawal (seizure), respiratory depression in combination Never abrupt discontinuation without medical supervision; avoid combining with opioids or alcohol Never advise abrupt cessation — medical taper required; consult prescriber; educate on withdrawal seizure risk; document all CNS depressants client is using

Legal & Ethical Considerations by Setting

SettingKey Considerations
Outpatient / Private Practice Greatest clinical flexibility. Confirm state law on fentanyl test strips and naloxone prescribing authority. 42 CFR Part 2 applies to SUD records. Document harm reduction rationale clearly.
Schools FERPA and minor consent laws apply. Most school settings have abstinence-based mandates — know your policy before discussing HR strategies. Referral to external HR services is generally permissible. Naloxone in schools is increasingly supported by state law — check your state.
Corrections Highly restrictive environment. MAT access is constitutionally required (Eighth Amendment / Estelle) but inconsistently implemented. Focus on release planning, naloxone linkage pre-release, and MAT continuation. Do not provide supplies that violate facility rules.
Community Health / FQHC Often most receptive to HR frameworks. SSP referrals, naloxone dispensing, and MAT linkage are typically within scope. Know local SSP locations. Assess housing and food security — structural harms intersect with substance use harms.
Duty to warn: Activated by identifiable threat to a third party — not by ongoing substance use. Harm reduction does not create duty to warn obligations. Consult state statute and supervisor when uncertain.

Mandatory reporting: Substance use alone does not trigger mandatory reporting for adults. Child welfare concerns with parental substance use may, depending on state law and specifics.

Naloxone Resources

Key Clinical Actions

  • Prescribe or co-prescribe naloxone for any patient receiving opioid prescriptions or with opioid use disorder.
  • Many states have standing orders allowing pharmacists to dispense without individual prescription — know your state's order and direct clients there.
  • Provide or refer for naloxone training: intranasal vs. auto-injector, rescue breathing, calling 911.
  • Include household members and close contacts in the naloxone conversation.

Naloxone Products

  • Narcan (naloxone HCl) nasal spray: 4 mg intranasal — most widely dispensed community form.
  • Kloxxado: 8 mg intranasal — for higher-potency opioid environments.
  • Evzio: Auto-injector with voice guidance — easy for bystanders.
  • Generic injectable naloxone: Often distributed by SSPs and community programs.
  • NEXT Distro / NEXT Naloxone: Mail-order naloxone in many states — nextdistro.org
