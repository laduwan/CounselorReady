Biopsychosocial Assessment Framework

The biopsychosocial model (Engel, 1977) remains the standard framework for comprehensive clinical assessment. A rigorous interview systematically addresses all three domains before formulating a diagnosis or treatment plan.

Biological Domain

  • Chief complaint and HPI
  • Psychiatric history (episodes, hospitalizations, prior diagnoses)
  • Medical history and current conditions
  • Current medications and supplements
  • Substance use history (onset, patterns, consequences)
  • Family psychiatric and medical history
  • Sleep, appetite, energy patterns
  • Neurological and developmental history

Psychological Domain

  • Cognitive functioning and insight
  • Emotional regulation and coping styles
  • Trauma history (type, age of onset, duration)
  • Attachment patterns and early relationships
  • Personality structure and defensive style
  • Suicidal and homicidal ideation history
  • Academic and occupational functioning
  • Treatment history and response

Social-Environmental Domain

  • Current living situation and support system
  • Relationship and marital/family history
  • Occupational and financial stressors
  • Legal history
  • Cultural identity and acculturation stressors
  • Religious and spiritual beliefs
  • Community resources and barriers
  • Recent life events and chronic stressors

Mental Status Examination (MSE)

The MSE is a structured, systematic observation of the client's current psychological functioning. It is both observational (what the clinician sees and hears) and interview-based (what the client reports). Every diagnostic assessment record should include all eight domains.

Appearance

Physical presentation, grooming, hygiene, dress appropriateness for context, apparent age vs. stated age, distinguishing features, body habitus.

Descriptors: well-groomed, disheveled, age-appropriate attire, malodorous, cachectic, well-nourished.

Behavior / Psychomotor Activity

Level of cooperation, eye contact, gait and posture, psychomotor agitation or retardation, abnormal movements (tics, tremors, tardive dyskinesia).

Descriptors: cooperative, guarded, agitated, psychomotor retardation, fidgeting, pacing.

Speech

Rate, rhythm, volume, fluency, spontaneity, and articulation. Note pressured speech, poverty of speech, dysarthria, or mutism.

Descriptors: normal rate/rhythm/volume, pressured, circumstantial, tangential, loud, soft, monotone.

Mood (Subjective)

The client's own words describing their internal emotional state. Always use quotation marks in documentation.

Example: "I've been feeling really down and empty."

Affect (Objective)

Clinician's observation of the client's expressed emotional tone. Assess: range (full, restricted, blunted, flat), intensity (normal, heightened, diminished), quality (euthymic, dysphoric, euphoric, irritable, labile, anxious), and mood-affect congruence.

Thought Process

The form and organization of thinking as revealed through speech. Assess logical coherence and goal directedness.

Descriptors: linear and goal-directed, circumstantial, tangential, loose associations, flight of ideas, thought blocking, perseveration, clang associations, word salad.

Thought Content

The actual ideas and preoccupations expressed. Assess for SI (with or without plan/intent/means), HI, paranoid ideation, obsessions, compulsions, phobias, magical thinking, ideas of reference, delusions (persecutory, grandiose, erotomanic, somatic, nihilistic).

Perceptual Disturbances

Presence, modality (auditory, visual, tactile, olfactory, gustatory), and character of hallucinations or illusions. Note command auditory hallucinations specifically. Distinguish from dissociative phenomena and pseudo-hallucinations.

Cognition

Orientation (person, place, time, situation), recent and remote memory, concentration and attention, abstract reasoning, and fund of knowledge. Screen with validated tools (e.g., MMSE, MoCA) when indicated.

Insight and Judgment

Insight: Degree of awareness of having an illness and understanding its impact (none, partial, full). Judgment: Capacity to make sound decisions, particularly in social and safety contexts. Assess with hypothetical questions or recent decision examples.

Structured Interview Approaches

SCID-5 (Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-5)

Gold-standard research and clinical diagnostic interview. Two versions: SCID-5-CV (Clinician Version, major diagnoses) and SCID-5-RV (Research Version, comprehensive). Follows DSM-5 diagnostic decision trees. Requires clinical training; typically 45–90 minutes. Best for complex presentations or when diagnostic precision is critical.

MINI International Neuropsychiatric Interview

Structured diagnostic interview covering 17 major Axis I disorders per DSM-5/ICD-10. Designed for efficiency (15–30 minutes); widely used in primary care and research. Less detailed than the SCID but highly sensitive for ruling diagnoses in or out. Validated across many languages and populations.

Clinical Interview vs. Structured Tools: Semi-structured clinical interviews preserve therapeutic rapport and allow follow-up probing that fully structured tools do not. Best practice integrates structured screening measures with clinician-guided, client-centered dialogue. Structured tools should supplement clinical judgment, not replace it.

DSM-5-TR Diagnostic Framework

Accurate diagnosis requires systematic application of the DSM-5-TR criteria set, not pattern-matching to chief complaints. The diagnostic process includes four steps:

  1. Identify the full symptom picture — duration, frequency, severity, and functional impairment across settings.
  2. Confirm exclusion criteria are met — rule out substance-induced etiology, general medical condition, and better-fitting diagnoses.
  3. Apply specifiers — severity (mild/moderate/severe), course (single episode, recurrent, in remission), and episode-specific specifiers (e.g., with anxious distress, with melancholic features, with psychotic features).
  4. Conduct differential diagnosis — systematically compare competing diagnoses against the full clinical picture before assigning a primary diagnosis.

Always document the rationale for diagnosis selection and differential ruling in the clinical record. A diagnosis code without documented rationale is ethically and legally insufficient.

Common Differential Diagnosis Pairs

Presentation Competing Diagnoses Key Distinguishing Features
Persistent low mood, anhedonia MDD vs. Bipolar II Disorder Lifetime history of hypomania (Bipolar II); family history; age of onset; antidepressant response (switching or cycling suggests bipolar spectrum)
Chronic worry, tension GAD vs. PTSD Trauma history and hyperarousal linked to specific events (PTSD); avoidance of trauma cues; intrusive re-experiencing distinguish PTSD from free-floating GAD worry
Inattention, impulsivity ADHD vs. Anxiety Disorder ADHD symptoms present before age 12 across multiple settings; anxiety-driven inattention is situation-specific and linked to worry; cognitive testing may clarify
Emotional dysregulation, unstable relationships BPD vs. Bipolar Disorder BPD: mood shifts tied to interpersonal triggers, minutes to hours; Bipolar: episode duration days to weeks, not exclusively interpersonal; identity disturbance more prominent in BPD
Social withdrawal, flattened affect Depression vs. Negative Symptoms of Schizophrenia Psychotic history, formal thought disorder, and course of illness; timeline of negative symptoms relative to psychotic episodes; response to antidepressants
Recurrent somatic complaints Somatic Symptom Disorder vs. Medical Etiology vs. Illness Anxiety Disproportionate concern relative to objective findings; degree of health anxiety; functional impairment; prior medical workup completeness

Cultural Formulation Interview (CFI)

The DSM-5-TR Cultural Formulation Interview is a 16-question clinician-administered tool designed to elicit the client's cultural perspective on their illness experience, meaning, and help-seeking. It addresses four domains:

  • Cultural definition of the problem — how the client and their community name and understand the problem
  • Cultural perceptions of cause, context, and support — explanatory models, stressors, and protective factors
  • Cultural factors affecting self-coping and past help-seeking — prior informal and formal care; barriers encountered
  • Cultural factors affecting current help-seeking — what the client wants from treatment; concerns about stigma or cultural mismatch

The CFI is not a scoring tool — it is a structured conversation that improves diagnostic accuracy, reduces clinician bias, and strengthens therapeutic alliance, particularly with clients from communities where Western diagnostic categories may not align with illness experience.

Documentation Best Practices

Every diagnostic record must include: (1) Chief complaint in client's own words; (2) Full symptom inventory with duration, severity, and functional impact; (3) Complete MSE; (4) Differential diagnosis reasoning; (5) DSM-5-TR code(s) with specifiers; (6) Risk assessment; (7) Clinician signature and credentials.
  • Use behaviorally specific, observable language — avoid vague terms like "appeared depressed" without MSE support.
  • Document what was ruled out and why, not only what was diagnosed.
  • Note when diagnosis is provisional (insufficient information) or deferred (pending further assessment).
  • Record all sources of collateral information used (family, prior records, outside providers).
  • Align diagnostic coding with the clinical narrative — a mismatch between codes and documentation creates billing and liability risk.
  • Update diagnoses as the clinical picture evolves; outdated diagnoses in the record without amendment are a liability.
  • Comply with state licensing board standards for record retention (typically 7 years for adults, longer for minors).
Ethical Note: Diagnosis carries significant consequences for clients — insurance, employment, custody, and self-concept. Apply diagnostic criteria rigorously, avoid over-diagnosis for billing convenience, and discuss diagnostic impressions with clients transparently as part of informed consent and the therapeutic process.
