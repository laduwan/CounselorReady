Two-Dimensional Model (Brennan, Clark & Shaver, 1998)

Adult attachment styles are distributed along two orthogonal dimensions: Attachment Anxiety (fear of abandonment / rejection) and Attachment Avoidance (discomfort with closeness / dependence). The four quadrants map to the four style categories.

Bowlby's Key Concepts — At a Glance

  • Attachment Behavioral System: Biologically based motivational system; goal = proximity to protective figure when threatened
  • Internal Working Models (IWMs): Mental representations of self + others formed through early relational experience; guide expectations and behavior across the lifespan
  • Safe Haven: Attachment figure as refuge and comfort during distress
  • Secure Base: Confidence in figure's availability that enables exploration and risk-taking
  • Separation Anxiety: Normal adaptive response to threatened separation; adaptive at moderate levels, pathological when disproportionate
  • Monotropy: Primary bond formed with one principal caregiver; hierarchy of secondary figures
  • Loss & Mourning: Four phases — numbing, yearning/searching, disorganization/despair, reorganization; attachment disruption is the heart of grief
  • Earned Security: IWMs can change via new relationships, therapy, and coherent narrative construction

Hazan & Shaver (1987) — Adult Romantic Attachment Descriptors

Hazan & Shaver extended Ainsworth's infant patterns to adult romantic relationships, using three self-report paragraphs. Clinically useful as a starting point for psychoeducation.

Secure

"I find it relatively easy to get close to others and am comfortable depending on them and having them depend on me. I don't often worry about being abandoned or about someone getting too close to me."

~59% of adults in original sample

Anxious / Ambivalent

"I find that others are reluctant to get as close as I would like. I often worry that my partner doesn't really love me or won't want to stay with me. I want to merge completely with another person, and this desire sometimes scares people away."

~11% of adults in original sample

Avoidant

"I am somewhat uncomfortable being close to others; I find it difficult to trust them completely, difficult to allow myself to depend on them. I am nervous when anyone gets too close, and often, love partners want me to be more intimate than I feel comfortable being."

~25% of adults in original sample

Top 5 Attachment-Informed Interventions

1
Corrective Relational Experience: Consistent, attuned, boundaried therapeutic presence that disconfirms negative IWMs through repeated experience. The relationship is the intervention. Effective across modalities; documented in Bowlby, Johnson, Siegel, Fosha.
2
Therapeutic Rupture and Repair: Deliberate naming and collaborative processing of misattunements and relational breaches. Each repair sequence builds new relational expectation and increases distress tolerance. Central to all attachment-based approaches (Safran & Muran, 2000).
3
EFT Cycle De-escalation (couples): Identify pursuer-withdrawer cycle; slow the cycle; access underlying primary attachment emotions (fear, grief, longing); restructure attachment bids toward proximity-seeking. High evidence base for relationship distress (Johnson, 2004; Wiebe & Johnson, 2016).
4
Mentalization / Reflective Functioning: Develop client's capacity to understand their own and others' mental states (thoughts, feelings, intentions). Reduces attachment-driven interpersonal reactivity; core of MBT (Bateman & Fonagy, 2004). Particularly indicated for BPD and disorganized attachment.
5
Coherent Narrative Construction: Support client in building an integrated, emotionally honest account of attachment history — neither idealized nor overwhelmed. Coherence (not positivity) correlates with earned security. Supported by psychodynamic, EMDR, and narrative exposure approaches.

3 Key Assessment Tools

Adult Attachment Interview (AAI) — George, Kaplan & Main (1985)
Format: Semi-structured interview (~60–90 min); requires specialized training for administration and coding. What it measures: State of mind with respect to childhood attachment; narrative coherence, not content. Output: Categorical classification: Autonomous (F), Dismissing (Ds), Preoccupied (E), Unresolved (U/d). Best for: Research, complex presentations, trauma evaluation, training contexts. Gold standard in attachment science.
Experiences in Close Relationships — Revised (ECR-R) — Fraley, Waller & Brennan (2000)
Format: 36-item self-report; ~5 min to complete. What it measures: Two dimensions — Attachment Anxiety (18 items; abandonment/rejection fears) and Attachment Avoidance (18 items; discomfort with closeness/dependence); 7-point Likert scale. Output: Dimensional scores; quadrant placement. Best for: Routine clinical intake, outcome tracking, psychoeducation starting point. Freely available for clinical/research use. Strong psychometric properties.
Relationship Scales Questionnaire (RSQ) — Griffin & Bartholomew (1994)
Format: 30-item self-report; ~5–7 min. What it measures: Attachment anxiety and avoidance dimensions yielding four prototype scores (Secure, Fearful, Preoccupied, Dismissing) based on Bartholomew's two-dimensional model of self and other. Output: Subscale scores; dominant prototype. Best for: Clinical settings where client-friendly language is needed; useful for psychoeducation conversations; less research-validated than ECR-R but practical and widely used.
Quick Clinical Memory Aid — The 2×2:   Secure = want & get closeness comfortably.   Preoccupied = want closeness, fear they won't get it.   Dismissing = don't want closeness, don't pursue it.   Fearful = want closeness, but it's also the threat.
