Attachment Theory in Adult Psychotherapy
Clinical Reference Handout

Assessment and Intervention  |  GA Integrated Therapeutic Perspectives LLC

Foundational Concepts (Bowlby)

Internal Working Models (IWMs)

Mental representations of self, attachment figures, and relationships formed through early caregiver interactions. IWMs operate largely outside conscious awareness and serve as templates that filter perception, guide expectations, and shape behavior in close relationships throughout the lifespan. In therapy, IWMs manifest as assumptions about the therapist's availability, reliability, and responsiveness.

Safe Haven & Secure Base

Safe haven: The attachment figure as a refuge during distress — a source of comfort and protection when the individual is frightened, ill, or overwhelmed. In adult therapy, the therapeutic relationship functions as a corrective safe haven.

Secure base: The confidence that the attachment figure is reliably available, which allows the individual to explore the world. In therapy, a secure therapeutic base enables clients to explore painful material and experiment with new behavior.

Proximity-Seeking & Protest Behavior

When the attachment system is activated by threat or distress, individuals are motivated to seek closeness to their attachment figure (proximity-seeking). If access is blocked or the figure is unresponsive, protest behavior emerges — anger, clinging, or despair. In adults, these patterns surface as relationship conflict, therapy dropout, or therapeutic ruptures.

Monotropy & Hierarchy

Bowlby proposed that infants form a primary attachment bond (monotropy) with one principal caregiver, and a ranked hierarchy of secondary figures. In adults, romantic partners typically become primary attachment figures. The loss or unavailability of a primary attachment figure constitutes the most significant psychological threat and drives many presenting complaints in adult psychotherapy.

Ainsworth's Strange Situation & Adult Parallels

Mary Ainsworth's Strange Situation procedure (1978) identified infant behavioral patterns that map reliably onto adult attachment patterns assessed by the Adult Attachment Interview (AAI; George, Kaplan & Main, 1985).

Infant Pattern (Strange Situation) Adult Category (AAI) Core Descriptor
Secure (B) Autonomous / Free-Secure (F) Coherent, balanced narrative; values attachment; integrates positive and negative experiences
Anxious-Resistant / Ambivalent (C) Preoccupied (E) Entangled, angry, or passive; difficulty moving on from attachment experiences; narrative is lengthy and confused
Avoidant (A) Dismissing (Ds) Normalizing or idealizing of childhood; minimizes importance of attachment; narrative is brief, lacks episodic memory support
Disorganized / Disoriented (D) Unresolved / Disorganized (U/d) Lapses in monitoring of reasoning or discourse around loss/abuse; indices of disorientation, dissociation, or absorption
Clinical Note: The AAI evaluates state of mind with respect to attachment, not the objective quality of childhood events. A client who experienced neglect but narrates it coherently may be assessed as autonomous-secure. Earned security through therapy or meaningful relationships is a documented phenomenon.

The Four Adult Attachment Styles

Secure

Core beliefs: "I am worthy of love. Others are trustworthy and available." Positive model of self; positive model of other.

Behavioral pattern: Comfortable with intimacy and interdependence; able to seek support when distressed; can tolerate separation; responds flexibly to relationship stress; recovers after conflict.

Presentation in therapy: Engages openly; forms alliance quickly; can explore difficult material; uses therapist as secure base; responds well to rupture-and-repair sequences; realistic expectations of therapy.

Anxious-Preoccupied

Core beliefs: "I am unworthy of love. Others are capable of closeness but may abandon me." Negative model of self; positive model of other.

Behavioral pattern: Hyperactivating attachment strategies; craves closeness; hypervigilant to rejection cues; difficulty self-regulating; may appear clingy, demanding, or emotionally volatile; jealousy; separation protest.

Presentation in therapy: May idealize therapist; distressed between sessions; frequent contact or emails; difficulty ending sessions; strong negative reaction to vacations or cancellations; may test the relationship.

Dismissing-Avoidant

Core beliefs: "I am self-sufficient and fine. Others are unreliable or intrusive." Positive model of self; negative model of other.

Behavioral pattern: Deactivating attachment strategies; minimizes vulnerability; values independence; emotionally constricted; discomfort with closeness; intellectualizes; may appear emotionally unavailable to partners.

Presentation in therapy: Reports no distress despite evidence of problems; difficulty identifying or labeling emotions; therapy attendance may be irregular; resistance to discussing childhood; may terminate prematurely when emotional content intensifies.

Fearful-Avoidant (Disorganized)

Core beliefs: "I am unworthy of love. Others are dangerous or unavailable." Negative model of self; negative model of other.

Behavioral pattern: Simultaneous desire for and fear of closeness; the attachment figure is also the source of threat (fright without solution); oscillates between approach and withdrawal; dissociative features; vulnerability to re-traumatization in relationships.

Presentation in therapy: Most complex therapeutic relationship; approach-avoidance oscillation; may dissociate during sessions; history of trauma; high risk of therapeutic ruptures; can confuse therapeutic closeness with threat.

Therapeutic Stance by Attachment Style

Style Primary Goal Stance Adjustments Pitfalls to Avoid
Secure Maintain alliance; support exploration Standard collaborative stance; comfortable pace; challenge when warranted Over-accommodating; underestimating client's capacity
Anxious-Preoccupied Downregulate attachment system; build self-efficacy Warm but boundaried; consistent structure; don't reinforce hyperactivation; model calm affect; address between-session contact proactively Becoming overly responsive to bids for reassurance; irregular boundaries; reactive to client's emotional escalation
Dismissing-Avoidant Increase emotional access; tolerate vulnerability Respect autonomy; move slowly toward emotional content; use psychoeducation; acknowledge defenses before exploring them; avoid premature emotional confrontation Pushing emotional processing too quickly; interpreting independence as resistance; colluding with intellectualization
Fearful-Avoidant Establish safety; titrate exposure to relational experience Slow, transparent, non-intrusive; name the therapeutic relationship explicitly; use trauma-informed lens; prioritize stabilization before processing; watch for dissociation Moving too fast; interpreting avoidance as non-engagement; missing dissociative responses; replicating unpredictability of early caregiving

Assessment Approaches

Adult Attachment Interview (AAI)

Semi-structured interview assessing state of mind with respect to childhood attachment (George, Kaplan & Main, 1985). Coding focuses on narrative coherence, not content. Requires specialized training. Gold standard in attachment research; increasingly used in clinical settings, particularly with complex presentations and trauma.

Experiences in Close Relationships (ECR-R)

36-item self-report instrument (Fraley, Waller & Brennan, 2000) measuring two dimensions: attachment anxiety (fear of abandonment/rejection) and attachment avoidance (discomfort with closeness/dependence). Widely used in research and clinical intake; quick to administer; yields dimensional rather than categorical scores.

Relationship Scales Questionnaire (RSQ)

30-item self-report (Griffin & Bartholomew, 1994) based on the two-dimensional model (anxiety × avoidance) yielding four attachment prototypes: secure, fearful, preoccupied, and dismissing. Useful for clinical conversations about attachment patterns; client-friendly language.

Clinical History Interview Indicators

  • Childhood caregiver availability, consistency, responsiveness
  • Early losses, separations, or abuse history
  • Pattern of romantic relationships (length, conflict style, endings)
  • Friendships and support-seeking behavior
  • Occupational patterns related to dependency/autonomy
  • Previous therapy experiences and reasons for termination

Evidence-Based Intervention Strategies

1
Provide a Corrective Relational Experience: The therapeutic relationship itself is the primary mechanism of change in attachment-focused therapy. Consistent availability, attuned responsiveness, transparency, and reliable repair of ruptures provides lived experience that disconfirms negative IWMs. This is not curative through insight alone but through repeated relational experience over time.
2
Explicit Exploration of Attachment Patterns: Use psychoeducation to help clients identify their attachment style and understand how IWMs formed in early relationships are being activated in current relationships (including the therapeutic one). Linking present patterns to historical context reduces shame and increases self-understanding.
3
Metacognitive Awareness: Help clients observe their own attachment-related thoughts and feelings in real time ("What happens inside you when you think I might be running late?"). Fostering the observing ego around attachment activations reduces automatic behavior and increases flexibility. Central to Mentalization-Based Treatment (MBT) and Emotionally Focused Therapy (EFT).
4
Therapeutic Rupture and Repair: Ruptures (misattunements, perceived abandonment, boundary violations) are inevitable and, when repaired collaboratively, are among the most powerful curative moments in attachment-focused work. Use ruptures as in-vivo opportunities to practice different ways of managing relational distress. Explicitly name and discuss what happened; model accountability without collapse.
5
Narrative Integration (Earned Security): Drawing from the AAI tradition, help clients construct a coherent, integrated narrative of their attachment history — acknowledging both positive and negative experiences, attributing causality appropriately, and integrating affect with cognition. Coherence, not idealization, is the goal. This process is supported by approaches such as EMDR, psychodynamic therapy, and narrative exposure therapy.
6
Emotionally Focused Therapy (EFT) Cycle De-escalation: In couples work, identify the pursuer-withdrawer (anxious-avoidant) interactional cycle driven by underlying attachment fears ("I'm not important to you" / "I can't do anything right"). Slow the cycle; access primary emotions; restructure attachment bids. Highly effective for couple distress with attachment underpinnings (Johnson, 2004).
Key Research Finding: The therapist's own attachment security — either dispositional or "earned" — predicts therapeutic alliance quality and client outcomes independent of treatment modality (Dozier et al., 1994). Attachment-informed supervision and personal therapy support therapist reflexivity around relational patterns.

Bowlby's Key Theoretical Constructs — Quick Summary

Attachment Behavioral System: Biologically based motivational system activated by threat; goal is proximity to protective figure.
Goal-Corrected Partnership: Mature attachment in which both partners adjust behavior based on the other's goals and perspective; emerges in middle childhood.
Separation Anxiety: Normal adaptive response to threatened or actual separation from attachment figure; pathological when disproportionate.
Grief & Mourning: Bowlby described four phases: numbing, yearning/searching, disorganization/despair, reorganization. Attachment disruption is the core of bereavement.
Continuity vs. Change: IWMs are relatively stable but can change — particularly through new attachment relationships, psychotherapy, and the therapeutic relationship itself.
Complementary Systems: Attachment, caregiving, and sexuality are distinct but interacting behavioral systems. Therapy activates all three; awareness of which system is active in session is clinically useful.
