Solution-Focused Brief Therapy
Clinical Reference Handout

Solution-Focused Brief Therapy in Community Mental Health  |  2026

Foundations & Origins

Solution-Focused Brief Therapy (SFBT) was developed in the 1970s and 1980s at the Brief Family Therapy Center in Milwaukee, Wisconsin. The primary architects were Steve de Shazer and Insoo Kim Berg, working alongside colleagues including Scott Miller, Eve Lipchik, and Michele Weiner-Davis. Influenced by the work of Milton Erickson and the Mental Research Institute (MRI) at Palo Alto, de Shazer and Berg observed that clients often spend substantial time elaborating on problems while rarely discussing what was already working in their lives.

The foundational insight was deceptively simple: examining exceptions to problems — moments when the problem was absent or less severe — was more therapeutically productive than detailed problem analysis. This shift gave rise to a coherent model that emphasizes client strengths, amplifies what is already working, and orients the therapeutic conversation toward a preferred future rather than a dissected past.

Scott Miller later contributed to SFBT's dissemination and integrated elements of the common factors literature, emphasizing the alliance, client resources, and feedback-informed care as central to brief treatment effectiveness.

Core Assumptions

Client as Expert

The client is the expert on their own life. The therapist is curious, not directive. Solutions are built from the client's existing knowledge, values, and resources — not imported from an external framework.

Focus on Solutions, Not Problems

Understanding the origin or mechanism of a problem is not required for change. Detailed problem analysis can inadvertently reinforce the problem narrative. Co-constructing solutions is more efficient and less stigmatizing.

Small Change Leads to Big Change

Change is non-linear and recursive. A small, concrete behavioral shift can ripple outward into the client's relationships and self-concept. SFBT does not require a dramatic transformation — it requires a next step.

Every Client Has Strengths & Resources

No matter how entrenched the presenting problem, clients bring competencies, past successes, and relational supports. The therapist's task is to make those visible and usable.

SFBT is not technique-driven optimism. It is a disciplined epistemological stance: the future is negotiated, not predicted; change is inevitable; and clients are competent to author their own solutions.

Key Techniques

The Miracle Question

Originally developed by Insoo Kim Berg, the Miracle Question projects the client into a future where the problem has been resolved without specifying the mechanism of change. This bypasses the client's problem-saturated thinking and invites detailed, sensory-level description of a preferred future. Standard wording: "Suppose tonight, while you are asleep, a miracle happens. The problem that brought you here is gone. But because you were asleep, you don't know the miracle happened. When you wake up tomorrow, what would be the first small thing you'd notice that tells you something is different?" Follow-up questions amplify detail: who else would notice, what would they see you doing differently, what would change in your relationships.

Scaling Questions

Scaling questions translate abstract experience into a concrete, actionable continuum (typically 0–10). They are versatile — clinicians use them to assess current functioning, establish goal states, measure confidence in change, track progress across sessions, and identify next steps. The key is always to ask what a one-point increase would look like behaviorally: "You said you're at a 4. What would a 5 look like? What would you be doing differently?" Scaling anchors can be customized (e.g., for children, a "feelings thermometer").

Exception Questions

Exception questions direct the client's attention to times when the problem was absent, less intense, or handled differently. These moments contain the seeds of the solution. "When in the last week did you notice even a small piece of what you just described in your miracle picture?" The therapist then amplifies the exception: who was there, what the client did differently, what it felt like. The implicit message is that the client has already demonstrated the capacity for change.

Coping Questions

Coping questions are especially useful with clients who report no exceptions and who are in crisis or high distress. Rather than looking for when things were better, coping questions honor survival and resilience: "Given everything you are dealing with, how have you managed to keep going?" or "What has stopped things from getting even worse?" This shifts the frame from helplessness to agency without minimizing the client's pain.

Relationship Questions

Relationship questions invite the client to adopt the perspective of significant others in their life. "If your partner were here, what would they say they noticed that tells them you're doing a little better?" These questions externalize progress, utilize the client's relational context, and can surface motivational information about what the client values in their relationships.

The EARS Technique

EARS is a structured follow-up sequence used at the start of subsequent sessions to consolidate and extend gains made between sessions.

Step Intent Sample Language
E — Elicit Draw out what went better since the last session "What's been better, even just a little bit, since we last met?"
A — Amplify Expand the description of the positive change "Tell me more about that. What exactly did you do? Who noticed?"
R — Reinforce Highlight client agency and attribute change to client effort "It sounds like you made a real decision to do something different. How did you manage that?"
S — Start Again Identify what else has improved and cycle through E-A-R again "What else has been better?" (returns to Elicit)

Client Types: Visitor, Complainant, Customer

De Shazer identified three positions clients may occupy relative to change. Identifying the client's current position guides the clinician's intervention stance. Importantly, these are not stable traits — clients frequently move across positions within and between sessions.

Type Characteristics Appropriate Clinician Response
Visitor Does not perceive a problem; present because someone else (court, parent, employer) required attendance; no complaint of their own Validate attendance; avoid pushing for goals; look for anything the client might find personally useful about the encounter; compliment rather than task
Complainant Identifies a problem and wants it resolved; attributes the problem to an external source (other person, circumstance); not yet ready to act on their own role Acknowledge and validate; use observation tasks only (e.g., "Notice when things are even slightly better"); avoid action-oriented homework
Customer Identifies a problem, perceives a personal role in the solution, and is ready to work toward change Full SFBT toolkit; collaboratively set goals; assign between-session tasks; use scaling and exception questions actively

Session Structure

  1. Problem-free talk (optional, brief): Begin with light conversation that establishes rapport and opens a view of the client outside the problem.
  2. Identifying what the client wants: "What would you like to leave here with today?" or "What needs to happen for this session to be worthwhile for you?"
  3. Goal negotiation: Goals should be small, specific, behavioral, stated in the positive (presence of something, not absence), and within the client's control.
  4. Exception exploration: Identify when the problem is less present; amplify and attribute the client's role in those exceptions.
  5. Scaling: Anchor current state and next step; assess confidence and motivation.
  6. Break (optional in brief community settings): Clinician takes a brief pause to formulate a message. In community mental health, this may be shortened or omitted.
  7. Compliments and task: Deliver genuine compliments grounded in what the client reported; offer a tailored between-session task (observation or action, matched to client type).

Applications in Community Mental Health

Community mental health settings present unique clinical and systemic conditions that make SFBT particularly well-suited as a primary treatment framework:

  • Brief treatment mandates: Managed care and agency protocols often limit sessions to 6–12 visits. SFBT is designed for brevity — the average treatment course in controlled studies is 4–8 sessions — making it a natural fit without requiring protocol modifications.
  • Diverse populations: SFBT's non-pathologizing, client-as-expert stance translates across cultures, presenting problems, and literacy levels. The approach avoids imposing a disease model, which can be alienating for clients from communities with historical distrust of mental health systems. Language can be adapted to the client's frame of reference with minimal model fidelity loss.
  • Mandated and reluctant clients: Community mental health caseloads frequently include court-ordered, child welfare-involved, or otherwise externally referred clients who present as visitors or complainants. SFBT's explicit framework for working with these client positions is a structural advantage over models that require voluntary motivation as a prerequisite.
  • Co-occurring presentations: SFBT integrates with harm reduction frameworks, trauma-informed care, and medication management without requiring exclusivity. Clinicians may use SFBT techniques within an eclectic or integrative frame.
  • Documentation and outcomes: Scaling questions generate session-by-session outcome data that maps directly onto behavioral health outcome measures (e.g., PHQ-9, GAD-7 trajectory), supporting billing and quality documentation requirements.
Supervision Note: SFBT fidelity does not mean avoiding problem talk entirely. When a client needs to be heard, the SFBT clinician listens — and then pivots toward strengths and exceptions. The directive to "focus on solutions" describes the overall orientation, not a rigid prohibition on acknowledging pain or difficulty.
