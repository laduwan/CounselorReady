SFBT Quick Reference Card

Solution-Focused Brief Therapy in Community Mental Health

SFBT Question Types with Sample Language

Question Type Purpose Sample Language
Miracle Question Projects client into a future without the problem; bypasses problem-saturated thinking; generates vivid, behavioral description of goals "Suppose tonight while you were sleeping a miracle happened and the problem that brought you here was gone. When you woke up tomorrow, what would be the very first thing you noticed that told you something was different?"
Scaling Questions Translates abstract experience into actionable 0–10 continuum; assesses current state, goal state, confidence, progress, and next step; generates session-to-session outcome data "On a scale of 0 to 10, where 10 is the miracle picture and 0 is the worst it's ever been, where would you say you are today?" / "What would a [X+1] look like? What would you be doing differently?"
Exception Questions Identifies times the problem was absent or less severe; surfaces existing strengths and solutions; attributes competence to client "Tell me about a time recently — even just a small moment — when things felt even slightly better, or closer to what you described." / "What was different about that time? What did you do that helped?"
Coping Questions Used when client reports no exceptions or is in high distress; honors survival and resilience without minimizing pain; shifts frame from helplessness to agency "Given everything you're dealing with, how have you managed to keep going?" / "What has prevented things from getting even worse?" / "What has kept you functioning through all of this?"
Relationship Questions Invites client to adopt perspective of significant others; externalizes and validates progress; surfaces relational motivation; useful for building between-session awareness "If your [partner/parent/friend] were here right now, what would they say they've noticed that tells them you're doing even a little better?" / "Who in your life would be least surprised by this change?"
Presupposing Change Uses language that assumes improvement is occurring; counters pessimistic thinking; embeds expectation of progress in the question itself "What's been better since we last met?" (vs. "Has anything been better?") / "What have you noticed that tells you things are starting to shift?"

Visitor / Complainant / Customer Model

Visitor

Profile: Does not self-identify a problem; present due to external requirement (court, employer, family pressure); no personal complaint

Interventions:

  • Validate attendance; do not argue about whether a problem exists
  • Look for anything personally useful the client might take from the session
  • Deliver genuine compliments; assign no homework
  • Ask: "Is there anything about your situation you'd like to be different, even if you don't think it's a problem?"

Complainant

Profile: Identifies a problem; attributes it to external source (another person, circumstances, bad luck); motivated to vent but not yet ready for personal action

Interventions:

  • Acknowledge and validate the complaint fully before redirecting
  • Use observation tasks only: "Between now and next time, notice when things are even slightly better"
  • Avoid action-oriented homework — premature tasks increase dropout
  • Exception questions can gently begin opening the door to agency

Customer

Profile: Identifies a problem, perceives personal agency, is ready to work toward change; most amenable to full SFBT toolkit

Interventions:

  • Full toolkit: miracle question, scaling, exceptions, relationship questions
  • Collaboratively negotiate concrete, behavioral, positive goals
  • Assign action or observation between-session tasks matched to stated goal
  • EARS at next session to amplify and reinforce gains
Important: These are positions, not personality types. A client can move from Visitor to Customer within a single session — or slide back. Re-assess at the start of each session.

SFBT Stance Reminders

Lead from behind. Stay curious, not directive. The client knows their own life.
Amplify exceptions, not problems. When you hear an exception, pause and dig — who, what, when, how.
Compliments are therapeutic, not social. Base them on what the client actually reported; vague praise is hollow.
Goals must be well-formed. Small. Specific. Behavioral. Stated in the positive. Within client's control. Co-constructed.
Problem talk is not forbidden. When a client needs to be heard, listen. Then orient toward strengths.
Solution ≠ opposite of problem. Help clients describe what they want, not just what they want to stop.
Assume competence. If you can't find an exception, ask a coping question — there is always something that has sustained the client.
Use the client's language. Reflect back their exact words; don't reframe into clinical jargon.

Community Mental Health Adaptations

CMH Context / Challenge SFBT Adaptation
Session limits (6–12 sessions) SFBT is designed for brevity (avg. 4–8 sessions); no adaptation needed. Orient to goals in session 1; use EARS to consolidate gains rapidly.
Mandated / involuntary clients Identify Visitor or Complainant position immediately; avoid goal-setting pressure; look for personal goals within the mandated context ("What would make this feel less like a waste of your time?")
Crisis presentations Use coping questions to stabilize; miracle question may be appropriate after safety is established to build forward vision; scaling for safety and hope.
Cultural and linguistic diversity Mirror the client's vocabulary for problems and solutions; avoid imposing wellness concepts that are culturally dissonant; relationship questions leverage collectivist values naturally.
Co-occurring substance use Pairs well with harm reduction; use scaling to assess readiness and confidence; exception questions surface abstinence or reduced-use periods as evidence of capacity.
Documentation / billing requirements Scaling scores map to measurable outcomes; exception-based goal language translates readily to behavioral health treatment plan language; track scale anchors session-to-session as progress indicators.
Managed care / medical model pressure SFBT goals and scaling can be reframed in symptom-reduction language for payers without abandoning the strengths-based clinical stance internally.
EARS (Follow-Up Sessions):
Elicit — "What's been better?"
Amplify — "Tell me more. What did you do?"
Reinforce — "That took real effort. How did you do that?"
Start Again — "What else has been better?"
Well-Formed Goal Checklist:
○ Small and achievable
○ Stated in the positive (presence of something)
○ Specific and behavioral (observable)
○ Situationally grounded (when, where, with whom)
○ Client's own words and values
○ Within the client's control
