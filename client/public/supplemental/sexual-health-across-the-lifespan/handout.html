Sexual Health Across the Lifespan

Assessment & Psychoeducation in Counseling — Clinical Quick-Reference

PLISSIT Model

Level Description Scope
P Permission Normalize; open the topic; signal safety to discuss sexuality
LI Limited Information Correct myths; provide psychoeducation within scope of training
SS Specific Suggestions Evidence-based strategies for identified concerns
IT Intensive Therapy Sex therapy / specialized treatment; refer when indicated

Annon (1976). Most LPCs/LCSWs practice at P & LI; SS requires additional training.

Biopsychosocial-Spiritual Framework

  • Biological: hormones, medications, chronic illness, neurological factors, pain, aging
  • Psychological: trauma history, body image, anxiety/depression, attachment style, cognitions
  • Social: relationship dynamics, culture, religion/spirituality, partner communication
  • Spiritual: values, meaning-making, religious guilt/shame, sexual ethics held by client

Clinical note: Assess all four domains before conceptualizing a sexual health concern. Biological causes are often missed when psychosocial history is rich.

Medication Effects on Sexual Function

  • SSRIs/SNRIs: delayed orgasm, decreased libido, arousal difficulties — most common
  • Antipsychotics: hyperprolactinemia → decreased desire, ED, anorgasmia
  • Antihypertensives: beta-blockers → ED; diuretics → lubrication issues
  • Hormonal contraceptives: may decrease HSDD, affect lubrication
  • Opioids: suppress testosterone; chronic use → hypogonadism
  • Anticonvulsants: reduced androgen levels; desire changes

Normalize discussion; coordinate with prescriber; never advise medication changes.

Desire Discrepancy

  • Difference in desired frequency or type of sexual activity between partners
  • Neither partner's desire level is pathological by default
  • Distinguish responsive vs. spontaneous desire (Basson model)
  • Common in long-term relationships; normalize without dismissing distress
  • Therapeutic focus: communication, needs negotiation, reducing pressure

Basson model: Many people (esp. women) experience desire after stimulation, not before. Responsive desire is normal — reframe "low libido" if initiation cues are absent.

Developmental Considerations

Adolescence (12–18)

  • Normative sexual exploration; identity formation (Erikson)
  • Assess: sexual activity, STI knowledge, consent understanding, LGBTQ+ identity
  • Psychoeducation: consent, healthy relationships, contraception (within scope + mandates)
  • Watch for: early sexual initiation linked to trauma, coercion, substance use

Young Adulthood (19–35)

  • Identity consolidation; romantic attachment formation
  • Common concerns: performance anxiety, sexual trauma processing, relationship conflict
  • Diversity of relationship structures (monogamy, ENM) — avoid assumptions

Middle Adulthood (36–60)

  • Perimenopause/menopause: GSM (genitourinary syndrome), decreased lubrication, desire shifts
  • Men: gradual testosterone decline; ED prevalence increases after 40
  • Relationship transitions: infidelity, divorce, repartnering
  • Chronic illness onset affects body image and sexual function

Older Adulthood (61+)

  • Sexuality does not end with aging — challenge ageist assumptions in sessions
  • Concerns: disability, partner loss, care facilities limiting sexual expression
  • STI rates rising in 65+ population; assess risk without assumptions
  • Cognitive decline: capacity to consent is an ethical and clinical issue

Assessment Tools

  • FSFI (Female Sexual Function Index) — 19-item validated scale; desire, arousal, lubrication, orgasm, satisfaction, pain
  • IIEF (International Index of Erectile Function) — 15-item; ED severity
  • SIDI (Sexual Interest and Desire Inventory–Female) — HSDD assessment
  • SFQ28 (Sexual Function Questionnaire) — broad multidomain measure
  • Brief Sexual Symptom Checklist (BSSC) — brief screen; flags for referral

Validated tools support clinical judgment; they do not replace a thorough clinical interview.

Ethical Obligations in Sexual Health

  • ACA A.4.b: Counselors avoid imposing personal values on clients; this applies explicitly to sexuality and relationships
  • Scope of practice: Psychoeducation and P/LI-level PLISSIT are within general scope; sex therapy techniques require specialized training
  • Dual-role risk: Sexual health counseling raises transference/countertransference intensity; supervision recommended
  • Minors: Developmentally appropriate sexual health information is protective; state law governs confidentiality for sexual health services to minors

When to Refer

Sex Therapist (AASECT-certified)

SS/IT-level concerns; paraphilias; vaginismus/penetration disorders; sexual trauma requiring EMDR/specialized protocol

Medical (OB/GYN, Urology, Endocrinology)

Physical pain, suspected hormonal etiology, ED without psychological precipitant, medication review needed

Psychiatry / Prescriber

Medication-induced sexual dysfunction; hypersexual disorder with psychiatric comorbidity; compulsive sexual behavior
