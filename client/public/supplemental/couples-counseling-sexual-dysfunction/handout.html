Couples Counseling for Sexual Dysfunction
Clinical Reference Handout

Couples Sexual History-Taking Template

Administer jointly or separately depending on relational safety. Normalize history-taking as routine clinical practice. Revisit over multiple sessions as trust develops.

Relationship & Sexual History

  • How long have you been together? When did sexual concerns first appear?
  • How did you meet, and what was intimacy like early in the relationship?
  • What changes in your sexual relationship have you noticed over time?
  • Has either of you experienced sexual trauma, abuse, or significant sexual loss? (assess individually first)
  • What were each of your family-of-origin messages about sexuality, bodies, and pleasure?
  • Have there been infidelity, betrayal, or trust ruptures in this relationship?

Current Sexual Functioning

  • How would each of you describe your current sex life — frequency, satisfaction, concern?
  • Who typically initiates? How does the other respond?
  • Are there physical symptoms: pain, difficulty with arousal, orgasm, or erection?
  • What medical conditions, medications, or substances might be affecting sexual response?
  • What have you tried so far? What has helped, what hasn't?
  • How does the sexual difficulty affect how you feel about yourself and about each other?

Sexual Communication Assessment Guide

Domain Assessment Questions What to Observe
Initiation Patterns "How do you each let the other know you're interested? How does your partner usually respond?" Directness, clarity, pursuit-withdrawal cycles, bid recognition
Consent & Refusal "When one of you isn't in the mood, how is that communicated? How does it land for the other?" Shame responses, avoidance, resentment, safety in saying no
Preference Disclosure "Are there things you'd like sexually that you haven't been able to ask for? What gets in the way?" Inhibition, vulnerability tolerance, past negative reactions to disclosure
Post-Encounter Processing "After sex — or after avoiding sex — what do you each feel? Do you talk about it?" Disconnection, shame, relief, closeness; repair capacity
Meta-Communication "Can you talk with each other about your sexual relationship outside of the moment?" Capacity to discuss sex at a relational level, not just in the act

Desire Discrepancy: Systemic Framework

Desire discrepancy is a relational pattern, not a pathology of the lower-desire partner. Both positions are shaped by individual, relational, and contextual factors.

Assessing the Lower-Desire Position

  • Baseline desire vs. desire in this relationship specifically
  • Medical and hormonal factors (fatigue, pain, medications)
  • Emotional safety and unresolved conflict as desire suppressants
  • Responsive vs. spontaneous desire model — is desire expected to arrive before arousal?
  • Resentment, role burden, or caregiver fatigue in the relationship

Assessing the Higher-Desire Position

  • Is sex serving as the primary vehicle for emotional closeness or reassurance?
  • How is rejection experienced? (Shame, abandonment, contempt?)
  • Does pursuit behavior inadvertently suppress partner's desire?
  • Are there other emotional needs that could be met outside of sex?
  • History of sexual anxiety, compulsivity, or attachment style influences

Treatment Framework Reference

Modality Core Application Key Intervention
Gottman Method Address sexual concerns through the lens of friendship, trust, and conflict management Build Love Maps for sexual identity; apply Sound Relationship House to intimacy; repair bids in sexual contexts
EFT (Johnson) Sexual vulnerability as attachment bid; dysfunction as protest or withdrawal Stage 1: de-escalate pursuer-withdrawer around sexual conflict; Stage 2: access and share primary emotions about sexuality and attachment
Sensate Focus (adapted) Reduce performance anxiety; restore non-goal-directed physical connection Progressive touch exercises with explicit ban on intercourse initially; debrief experience rather than outcome
Sexual Genogram Map intergenerational sexual scripts, shame, and messages in both partners Three-generation exploration of family attitudes toward bodies, pleasure, gender roles, and sexual secrecy
Individual vs. Couples Format: Consider concurrent individual sessions when there is undisclosed trauma, an active affair, significant shame that inhibits disclosure in the couple's presence, or one partner's ambivalence about the relationship. Coordinate to maintain appropriate confidentiality boundaries.
