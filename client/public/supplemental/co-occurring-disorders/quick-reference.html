Treatment Models Comparison

Model Description Best For Limitations
Sequential Treat SUD first; address MHD after stabilization (or vice versa). One condition at a time. Clients with mild MHD symptoms clearly driven by acute substance use; substance-induced presentations MHD symptoms worsen untreated; clients rarely reach "phase 2"; systems don't communicate; high dropout; poor outcomes for true COD
Parallel Both disorders treated simultaneously but by separate providers in separate systems without coordination. When integrated care is unavailable; client preference for separate specialized providers Conflicting treatment messages; client navigates two systems alone; no shared treatment plan; medication recommendations may conflict; 42 CFR Part 2 disclosure barriers
Integrated (IDDT) SUD and MHD treatment delivered by same team with unified goals, shared records, and stage-matched interventions. All true COD presentations; clients with trauma + SUD; serious mental illness + SUD; repeated treatment failures in sequential/parallel systems Requires cross-trained staff; higher organizational investment; documentation complexity (42 CFR Part 2); not universally available in all markets

Common COD Pairings — Interaction Patterns & Clinical Considerations

SUD + MHD Interaction Pattern Clinical Consideration
Alcohol + MDD Alcohol (CNS depressant) provides short-term dysphoria relief; chronic use deepens depression; depressive episodes increase relapse risk Reassess MDD at 2–4 weeks abstinence — many depressive symptoms remit. Naltrexone + antidepressant combinations are evidence-supported.
Stimulants + Bipolar Stimulants precipitate manic/hypomanic episodes; mania increases stimulant-seeking behavior; stimulant withdrawal mimics depressive episode Mood stabilization is a treatment priority. Stimulant withdrawal depression often resolves in 2–3 weeks without antidepressants — avoid premature diagnosis.
Cannabis + Anxiety Short-term anxiolytic effect drives cannabis use; chronic use increases anxiety sensitivity; cannabis-induced anxiety disorder is a recognized presentation Paradoxical relationship: client often insists cannabis helps anxiety while anxiety scores increase. Psychoeducation on acute vs. chronic effects is essential.
Opioids + PTSD Opioids blunt hyperarousal and emotional numbing; PTSD symptoms intensify in withdrawal; trauma relapse cues increase SUD relapse risk Integrated trauma-informed care alongside MAT. EMDR and CPT deliverable with stable MAT. Avoid trauma processing during acute withdrawal or intoxication.
Alcohol/Sedatives + PTSD Substance suppresses nightmares and hyperarousal; withdrawal-phase rebound dramatically intensifies trauma symptoms Safety planning during detox is critical. Benzodiazepine taper requires psychiatric coordination. Psychoeducate about rebound anxiety to prevent early dropout.
Any SUD + Psychosis / Schizophrenia Cannabis, stimulants can precipitate psychotic episodes; alcohol/benzodiazepine use complicates antipsychotic effects; poverty of motivation common ACT (Assertive Community Treatment) team model is the evidence-based approach. Engagement focus over confrontation. Medication adherence support is central.

Stages of Change × Intervention Approach

Stage Client Characteristics Evidence-Based Intervention Approach
Pre-Contemplation Does not recognize problem; denies COD relationship; may be mandated Build rapport without confrontation; raise awareness using reflective listening; psychoeducation on SUD-MHD interaction; express genuine curiosity about client's theory of their situation; sow seeds of doubt without argument
Contemplation Acknowledges problem but ambivalent about change; weighing pros/cons Decisional balancing; elicit and reflect change talk; explore the connection between SUD and MHD symptoms ("What do you notice about your anxiety the day after drinking?"); do NOT push for action; normalize ambivalence as part of the process
Preparation Intends to change; beginning to plan; may have set a target date Help client identify concrete steps; discuss integrated treatment options including MAT; address barriers proactively; strengthen self-efficacy; involve natural supports if appropriate; develop preliminary crisis plan
Action Actively making behavior changes; high-effort phase Skills-based interventions (CBT, DBT, CPT, EMDR); relapse prevention planning for SUD AND MHD separately; medication management support; coping skills practice; connect to peer support; crisis planning; address lifestyle factors (sleep, exercise, nutrition)
Maintenance Sustained change; working to prevent relapse; may reduce contact Identify high-risk situations for both disorders; develop long-term coping repertoire; peer support and community integration; ongoing monitoring; address meaning and identity in recovery; plan for seasonal/anniversary triggers
Relapse Return to substance use or psychiatric decompensation; shame and demoralization common Frame as expected part of the nonlinear process; conduct functional analysis; reassess current stage; adjust treatment plan accordingly; reinforce therapeutic alliance; evaluate safety; affirm prior gains as still real

IDDT Fidelity Principles

  1. Integration: Same team delivers both SUD and MHD services
  2. Comprehensiveness: Addresses full range of client needs (housing, vocational, social, medical)
  3. Stage-wise treatment: Interventions matched to client's stage for each disorder independently
  4. Assertive outreach: Team goes to client when client cannot engage program
  5. Motivational interviewing: Non-confrontational; engagement-focused throughout engagement and persuasion stages
  6. Harm reduction: Reduction is a legitimate clinical goal; abstinence is not the only acceptable outcome
  7. Multiple psychotherapies: CBT, DBT, ACT, trauma-focused modalities used based on presentation and stage
  8. Long-term perspective: Recovery measured in years; brief treatment episodes are insufficient
  9. Optimism and hope: Team maintains recovery-oriented stance; relapse is expected, not failure
  10. Medication: MAT and psychiatric medications are offered, not withheld pending sobriety
  11. Family psychoeducation: Family systems involved with client consent; family members educated about COD
  12. Social support: Development of non-substance-using social network is a treatment goal

Screening Tools — Cutoff Reference

Substance Use Screening

AUDIT (10 items; 0–40) Score 1–7: Low risk
Score 8–14: Hazardous use
Score 15–19: Harmful use
Score ≥20: Probable AUD
DAST-10 (10 items; 0–10) Score 0: No problem
Score 1–2: Low problem
Score 3–5: Moderate problem
Score 6–8: Substantial problem
Score 9–10: Severe problem
AUDIT-C (3-item brief screen) Men: Score ≥4 = positive screen
Women: Score ≥3 = positive screen

Mental Health Screening

PHQ-9 (9 items; 0–27) Score 1–4: Minimal
Score 5–9: Mild
Score 10–14: Moderate
Score 15–19: Mod-severe
Score 20–27: Severe
GAD-7 (7 items; 0–21) Score 0–4: Minimal
Score 5–9: Mild
Score 10–14: Moderate
Score ≥15: Severe
PCL-5 (20 items; 0–80) Score ≥31–33: Probable PTSD (cutoff varies by setting)
Diagnosis requires clinical interview; screening score alone is insufficient
Key reminders for COD practice: (1) Screening scores may be inflated by substance-induced symptoms — reassess MHD at 2–4 weeks of sustained abstinence when clinically feasible. (2) Positive screens indicate need for comprehensive assessment, not a diagnosis. (3) For PTSD + SUD, evidence supports treating both concurrently — stabilization does not require complete abstinence before trauma processing can begin. (4) 42 CFR Part 2 may restrict SUD record disclosure even within integrated systems — consult your compliance officer on record segregation practices.
